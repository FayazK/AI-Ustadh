# Scientific Research — Honey (شہد)
**Fetched:** 2026-05-29

> Scope: science spine for a Quran/hadith podcast episode connecting An-Nahl (16:68–69) and the medicinal tradition of honey with modern evidence. Findings are weighted by evidence quality. Strong/established facts are separated from preliminary or disputed claims in the final section. Nothing here should be read as medical advice.

## 1. Composition

Honey is roughly **80% sugars and ~17% water**, with the remaining ~3% made up of enzymes, acids, minerals, amino acids, vitamins, and antioxidant phenolics. Carbohydrates account for about **95% of honey's dry weight**.

- **Sugars (monosaccharides dominate):** Fructose averages ~**38%** and glucose ~**31%**, giving a typical **fructose/glucose (F/G) ratio of ~1.2** (varies roughly 0.4–1.6 by floral source). A higher fructose share is why honey tastes sweeter than table sugar and why honeys with more fructose stay liquid longer (glucose-rich honeys crystallise faster).
- **Other sugars:** Sucrose is normally **~1% (usually under 5%)** — high sucrose flags immature or adulterated honey. Maltose and other di-/oligosaccharides make up several percent.
- **Water:** ~**15–20%** (avg ~17%). Above ~18–20% honey risks fermentation, so low water content is a built-in preservative.
- **Enzymes** (added by the bees, not the flower):
  - **Invertase (α-glucosidase/sucrase):** splits nectar's sucrose into glucose + fructose.
  - **Diastase (amylase):** breaks down starch; used as a freshness/over-heating marker (it degrades with heat and age).
  - **Glucose oxidase:** converts glucose into **gluconic acid + hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)** — central to honey's acidity and antibacterial action.
  - **Catalase:** breaks H₂O₂ back into water and oxygen, regulating peroxide levels.
- **Acidity:** Naturally acidic, **pH ~3.4–6.1 (avg ~3.9)**; gluconic acid is the main organic acid. The low pH itself inhibits many bacteria.
- **Minerals:** Total ash ~0.2% (range 0.02–1.0%); **potassium** is the most abundant (~⅓ of minerals), with calcium, magnesium, sodium, iron, zinc, copper, manganese. Darker honeys carry more minerals.
- **Amino acids/proteins:** Low total protein (~0.2–0.5%); **proline** is the dominant amino acid (~50–85% of total) and is used as a ripeness/authenticity marker.
- **Vitamins:** Small amounts of B-complex (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6) and vitamin C.
- **Antioxidant phenolics:** **Flavonoids** (quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin, apigenin, chrysin, galangin, pinocembrin, pinobanksin) and **phenolic acids** (gallic, caffeic, ferulic, p-coumaric, chlorogenic, ellagic). Content tracks colour — **darker honey = more phenolics = higher antioxidant capacity**.

*(Source: "Honey: Chemical Composition, Stability and Authenticity," Int. J. Mol. Sci. / PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6017492/ ; "Honey as a Source of Dietary Antioxidants," Nutrients / PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6273711/ )*

## 2. Antibacterial / Wound-Healing

Honey kills or inhibits bacteria through **several mechanisms acting at once**, which is why — notably — **no confirmed bacterial resistance to honey has been documented**, unlike single-target antibiotics.

- **Osmotic effect:** Very high sugar + low water draws water out of bacterial cells (osmosis), dehydrating and inhibiting them.
- **Low pH (~3.2–4.5):** acidic enough to suppress bacteria that prefer near-neutral pH (~7.2–7.4).
- **Hydrogen peroxide:** glucose oxidase generates H₂O₂ slowly when honey is diluted (e.g., by wound fluid), giving sustained, low-level antisepsis without the tissue damage of concentrated peroxide. This is the main mechanism in most ordinary honeys.
- **Methylglyoxal (MGO) — Manuka honey's special factor:** Manuka honey (from **Leptospermum scoparium**, NZ/Australia) gets its **non-peroxide** activity from **MGO**, formed non-enzymatically from **dihydroxyacetone (DHA)** in manuka nectar. MGO content underlies the **UMF** and **MGO** grading numbers.
- **Bee defensin-1:** an antimicrobial peptide the bees add, active especially against Gram-positive bacteria.

Honey shows lab activity against a broad range including **Staphylococcus aureus (incl. MRSA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, E. coli, and H. pylori**.

**Clinical translation:** **Medical-grade honey** dressings (e.g., **Medihoney**, often manuka-based) are gamma-irradiated to remove *Clostridium botulinum* spores and are **regulatory-cleared (e.g., FDA-cleared) as wound/burn dressings**. The Cochrane review on honey for wound healing found honey may help **partial-thickness burns** heal faster than conventional dressings, but evidence for many other wound types is of **low/uncertain quality and mixed**. Regulators (FDA, EFSA) have **not approved general systemic health claims** for honey and FDA has issued warning letters over unsupported disease-treatment claims.

*(Sources: "The Antibacterial Activity of Honey," Antibiotics / PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7997833/ ; Manuka honey overview, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuka_honey ; Cochrane "Honey for wound healing," https://www.cochrane.org/CD005083/WOUNDS_honey-for-wound-healing )*

## 3. Cough & Sore Throat

The **Cochrane review "Honey for acute cough in children" (Oduwole et al., 2018)** is the strongest evidence here:

- Honey **probably reduces cough symptoms** (frequency, severity, and sleep disturbance for child and parents) **more than no treatment, placebo, and diphenhydramine**.
- Honey is roughly **comparable to dextromethorphan** (little or no difference) and may beat **salbutamol** for cough.
- **GRADE certainty: moderate to low** — a genuine but modest benefit, and a reasonable, low-risk option parents can try.
- Trials covered children **roughly 1–18 years**.

This is consistent with why honey is a sensible alternative to over-the-counter cough syrups, which are not recommended for young children.

**CRITICAL SAFETY RULE — infant botulism:** **Honey must NOT be given to children under 12 months.** Honey can contain dormant *Clostridium botulinum* spores; an infant's immature gut can let them germinate and produce toxin, causing **infant botulism**. This single hard rule should anchor any discussion of honey's healing properties for children.

*(Source: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, Oduwole et al. 2018, via PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6513626/ )*

## 4. Honey vs Table Sugar (Sucrose)

**Chemically, honey and table sugar are close cousins.** Table sugar is sucrose — a single disaccharide that is ~50% glucose / 50% fructose chemically bonded together. Honey is essentially those **same two sugars already separated (free fructose + free glucose)**, plus water and trace compounds. The body ends up processing them very similarly.

- **Calories:** Honey is actually **slightly more calorie-dense per spoon** because it's heavier and contains less air/water than crystalline sugar. MD Anderson cites **~64 kcal/tablespoon for honey vs ~49 kcal/tablespoon for table sugar**. (Per *gram* they're similar; honey just packs more into a spoon.) Because honey tastes sweeter, you may use a bit less.
- **Glycemic index (GI, glucose = 100):** Honey's GI is **variable and typically moderate (~average ~58, with a wide range ~35–74 depending on floral source and its fructose/glucose ratio)**. Table sugar/sucrose sits around **~60–65**, pure glucose is **100**, and pure fructose is low (~15–20). So honey is often **modestly lower-GI than table sugar**, but not dramatically so, and high-glucose honeys can be as high as sugar.
- **Bottom line:** Honey carries **small amounts of antioxidants and trace minerals that refined sugar lacks**, but the amounts are minor — you'd have to eat unhealthy quantities to get a meaningful dose. Health authorities (Mayo Clinic, MD Anderson, Harvard) converge on the same message: **honey is "still sugar," counts as added sugar, and should be limited** like any sweetener. The American Heart Association's added-sugar caps (~6 tsp/day women, ~9 tsp/day men) **include honey**.

*(Sources: MD Anderson, "Are there health benefits to honey? Yes, but it's still sugar," https://www.mdanderson.org/cancerwise/are-there-health-benefits-to-honey--yes--but-its-still-sugar.h00-159698753.html ; Mayo Clinic, "Is honey better for you than sugar?", https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/expert-answers/honey/faq-20058038 ; Harvard Health glycemic index list, https://www.health.harvard.edu/diseases-and-conditions/glycemic-index-and-glycemic-load-for-100-foods )*

## 5. Honey and Diabetes — (handle with care; popular myth flagged)

**MYTH TO BUST:** "Honey is natural, so it's safe / free for diabetics." **This is NOT supported by mainstream guidance.** Honey is a sugar; it raises blood glucose and must be counted as a carbohydrate.

What the evidence actually shows — a balanced picture:

- **It raises blood sugar.** Honey is ~80% sugar; eating it elevates blood glucose like other sugars. Its moderately lower GI than sucrose does **not** make it a "free food."
- **Diabetes-management bodies treat it as sugar.** People with diabetes should **count honey toward total carbohydrate/added-sugar intake and limit it** — not assume it's exempt (MD Anderson; American Diabetes Association classifies it among caloric sweeteners that affect blood glucose).
- **Some research shows modest, mixed metabolic signals.** Controlled-feeding studies and meta-analyses report that honey — especially **raw, monofloral honeys (robinia/acacia, clover)** consumed **as a replacement for other sugars** at moderate doses — can produce **small reductions in fasting glucose and lipids** (see §8). But the **certainty is low**, effects are modest, and they appear in the context of **substituting** honey for other sugar, **not adding** it.
- **The honest synthesis:** If someone with diabetes is going to use a sweetener anyway, a small amount of honey *in place of* table sugar is a defensible swap — but it is still sugar, still counted, still limited, and **not therapeutic**. The popular claim that diabetics can eat honey freely is unsupported and potentially harmful.

*(Sources: "The relationship between honey and diabetes mellitus: A review," PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10692106/ ; American Diabetes Association on carbs/sweeteners, https://diabetes.org/food-nutrition/understanding-carbs/get-to-know-carbs ; MD Anderson, https://www.mdanderson.org/cancerwise/are-there-health-benefits-to-honey--yes--but-its-still-sugar.h00-159698753.html )*

## 6. The Honeybee — Instinct, Communication & How Honey Is Made

Connects directly to **An-Nahl 16:68–69**: the bee "inspired" (أوحى) to take homes in mountains, trees, and trellises, and to follow the paths of its Lord — producing a drink of "varying colours" in which is "healing for people."

**The waggle dance (decoded by Karl von Frisch; Nobel Prize in Physiology/Medicine, 1973, shared with Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen):**
- A returning forager performs a figure-eight "waggle run" on the vertical comb in the dark hive to tell nestmates **where food (or water, or a new nest site) is**.
- **Direction is encoded as an angle:** the angle of the waggle run relative to straight-up (gravity/vertical) equals the angle of the food source relative to the **sun's azimuth**. Straight up = "fly toward the sun." Bees even **compensate for the sun's movement** across the sky over time.
- **Distance is encoded in duration:** the **longer the waggle run, the farther the food**.
- **Quality/profitability** is signalled by how vigorously and how many times she dances.
- A simpler **round dance** signals food that is very close (limited directional info); the waggle dance is for more distant sources.
- Followers read the dance by touch and vibration in the dark. Modern automated tracking and even robotic-bee experiments have **confirmed** that bees act on this directional/distance information.

**Division of labour & who makes honey:**
- The colony is overwhelmingly **female worker bees** doing the foraging, comb-building, nursing, and **honey-making** — consistent with the **feminine grammatical form used for the bee in An-Nahl 16:68–69** (a frequently noted Quran–science connection).
- Workers progress through age-based roles (cleaning → nursing → comb-building → guarding → foraging).

**How honey is made (nectar → enzymes → dehydration):**
1. Foragers suck floral **nectar** into a "honey stomach," where enzymes (esp. **invertase**) begin converting sucrose into glucose + fructose.
2. Back at the hive, the nectar is passed mouth-to-mouth between bees, adding more enzymes (including **glucose oxidase**) and reducing water.
3. Bees deposit it in wax cells and **fan their wings to evaporate water** down to ~17–18%.
4. When ripe, the cell is capped with wax — the low water, low pH, and H₂O₂ keep it stable almost indefinitely.

*(Sources: Waggle dance, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waggle_dance ; Karl von Frisch — Nobel Prize facts, https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1973/frisch/facts/ ; "How do bees make honey?", National Geographic, https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/how-do-bees-make-honey ; "How Bees Make Honey," Dadant, https://www.dadant.com/learn/how-bees-make-honey/ )*

## 7. "Varying Colours / Shifa" — Colour, Source, and Therapeutic Reality

- **Colour comes from floral source** (and mineral/phenolic content): acacia/robinia and clover honeys are pale; buckwheat, manuka, and forest/honeydew honeys are dark. This is the literal, observable basis of the Quranic phrase "drink of varying colours" (مختلف ألوانه).
- **Darker honeys generally have higher phenolic/antioxidant content** — so colour is a rough proxy for antioxidant strength, not just appearance.
- **Documented, evidence-based therapeutic uses:** topical wound/burn care (esp. medical-grade/manuka) and symptomatic relief of acute cough in children (§2–§3) are the best-supported. Antioxidant and mild antibacterial properties are real and measurable.
- **Folklore vs evidence:** broad claims that ingested honey *cures* systemic diseases, "boosts immunity," or treats diabetes are **not established** by quality clinical trials and are not endorsed by regulators (FDA/EFSA). The Quranic "shifa" (healing) is best presented as encompassing genuine documented benefits **without** over-claiming modern cures honey hasn't been shown to deliver.

*(Sources: PMC6273711 antioxidants/colour, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6273711/ ; PMC7997833 antibacterial, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7997833/ ; Manuka honey/regulatory claims, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuka_honey )*

## 8. Notable Recent Studies & Reviews (2023–2026)

- **Sievenpiper group, *Nutrition Reviews* (2023) — "Effect of honey on cardiometabolic risk factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis."** Pooled **18 controlled feeding trials (~1,105 participants, median ~40 g/day, ~8 weeks)**. Honey produced a **modest reduction in fasting glucose (~−0.20 mmol/L pooled)** plus reductions in **total and LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, a fatty-liver marker (ALT), and fasting insulin**, with neutral/favourable HDL. Benefits were **stronger for raw honey and monofloral robinia (acacia) and clover** honeys; processing diminished them. **GRADE certainty: low.** The authors stress honey **replacing** other sugars (not added) at moderate doses, and that it remains a sugar — results should **not** be read as license to eat more sugar. (PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36408647/ ; Oxford Academic, https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/81/7/758/6855912 ; U of T summary, https://www.utoronto.ca/news/does-honey-have-benefits-new-study-finds-it-can-improve-some-measures-cardiometabolic-health )
- **Umbrella review of systematic reviews/meta-analyses on honey health outcomes (2024), PMC.** Across many reviews, honey's most consistent benefits were for **cough** and **some lipid/glycemic markers**, but **overall evidence quality was low to very low**, with high heterogeneity. Conclusion: honey is **not a treatment for disease**, remains an added sugar, and needs better-quality RCTs. (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11305085/ )
- **Honey, gut microbiota & cardiometabolic health RCT (2023), PMC** — explores whether honey's oligosaccharides/phenolics act prebiotically; early-stage, small, hypothesis-generating. (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10663978/ )
- **Manuka/medical-grade honey antibacterial mechanism studies (2022–2024)** continue to confirm activity against antibiotic-resistant bacteria in vitro and support its niche in wound care, while cautioning against extrapolating to systemic cures. (e.g., PMC8767536, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767536/ )

## Evidence-Quality Notes

**Well-established (high confidence):**
- Honey is ~80% sugar (fructose + glucose, F/G ~1.2) + ~17% water; composition and enzymes (invertase, diastase, glucose oxidase, catalase) are well characterised.
- Low water, low pH (~3.4–4.5), high osmolarity, and enzymatic H₂O₂ give honey genuine antibacterial activity; manuka's potency comes from MGO.
- Honey must **never** be given to infants under 12 months (botulism risk).
- Honey is chemically and metabolically "still sugar," counts as added sugar, and raises blood glucose; it is only marginally lower-GI than table sugar and not meaningfully more nutritious in normal amounts.
- Darker honey = more phenolics/antioxidants; colour is set by floral source.
- The waggle dance encodes direction (angle to sun) and distance (run duration); von Frisch's decoding earned the 1973 Nobel and is independently confirmed.
- Honey is made by **female worker bees** via nectar + enzymes + dehydration.

**Moderately supported (real benefit, moderate-to-low certainty):**
- Honey reduces acute cough symptoms in children >1 yr (Cochrane: moderate–low certainty).
- Medical-grade/manuka honey aids partial-thickness burns and certain wounds (Cochrane: helpful for burns, mixed/low for other wounds).

**Preliminary / modest / context-dependent (low certainty — do not overstate):**
- Honey's small improvements in fasting glucose, cholesterol, triglycerides, and liver enzymes (Sievenpiper 2023) — modest, low-certainty, mainly when **raw/monofloral honey replaces other sugar**, not as an add-on.
- Prebiotic/gut-microbiota effects — early, small studies.

**Disputed / unsupported (myths to flag):**
- "Honey is safe/free for people with diabetes" — **NOT supported**; honey is sugar and must be counted and limited.
- Honey as a *cure* for systemic diseases or a reliable "immune booster" — **not established**; FDA/EFSA reject such general health claims.
- "Honey is a guilt-free healthy sweetener you can use freely" — **false**; it's an added sugar subject to the same limits.
