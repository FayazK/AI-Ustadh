# Women's Parda / Hijab & Libas — History and Dress Reference

**Topic key:** `hijab-libas-women`

## Headline Findings (for quick reference)

1. **The single most striking pre-Islam → Islam contrast:** The head-cloth (*khimar*) **already existed** in Jahiliyyah Arabia — but women wore it **thrown back behind the head/shoulders**, with the front shirt-opening left loose so the **neck, upper chest, and jewelry were displayed** (the practice of *tabarruj*). The Quran (24:31) did not invent the cloth; it **redirected** it — commanding women to *draw it forward over the juyub* (the neck-and-bosom opening). So the shift was **from display to concealment of the same garment**: the khimar moved from the back to the front.

2. **Key evidence veiling predated and spread beyond Arab culture:** Veiling is attested in **Middle Assyrian law (c. 1400–1100 BCE)**, where it was *legally mandated for respectable free women and legally forbidden to slaves and prostitutes* — roughly **2,000 years before Islam**. It is documented in **ancient Greece, Rome, the Byzantine Christian world, and Sasanian Persia** as a marker of status and respectability. Veiled-priestess statuettes date to ~2500 BCE. The hijab is therefore a **Near Eastern / Late-Antique inheritance given a new moral framing by Islam** — emphatically *not* a "desi" or purely-Arab cultural invention.

3. **One principle, many cultural forms (regional garment names):** abaya (Arab), chador (Persian/Iran), burqa & paranja (Afghanistan / Central Asia), niqab (Arabian Peninsula), dupatta / chador / ghoonghat-style (South Asia), haik + aadjar & sefseri (North Africa / Maghreb), tudung / kerudung / baju kurung (Southeast Asia). All are *cultural expressions of one Quranic principle* — which refutes "parda is just a desi cultural thing."

## A. Pre-Islamic (Jahiliyyah) Arabian Women's Dress & Customs

### A.1 — The khimar already existed, but was worn BACKWARD (the core point)

Classical tafseer is explicit that Arabian women in the *Jahiliyyah* **already owned and wore a head-cloth (khimar)** — the command in 24:31 was not "start covering your head" but "**stop wearing it thrown back and instead draw it forward over your bosom.**"

- **Ibn Kathir (on 24:31):** *Khumur* is the plural of *khimar*, "which means something that covers… what is used to cover the head." The command to draw the veils *over their juyub* means "over their necks and chests so that nothing can be seen of them." Pre-Islamic women, by contrast, "would pass in front of men with their chests completely uncovered, and with their necks, forelocks, hair and earrings uncovered." (Tafsir Ibn Kathir, 24:31.)

- **Maududi / classical commentators (on 24:31):** "In the pre-Islamic days of ignorance, women used to wear a sort of head-band, which was tied in a knot at the rear of the head. The slit of the shirt in the front partly remained open, exposing the front of the neck and the upper part of the bosom." After the verse, "the head-wrapper… was introduced among the Muslim women, which was meant to cover the head, the breasts, and the back, completely." [secondary: tafseer aggregators]

**Why 24:31 phrases it as "draw it OVER the juyub":** The *juyub* (sing. *jayb*) is the **opening/slit at the front of the shirt over the bosom**. The instruction *wal-yadribna bi-khumurihinna 'ala juyubihinna* literally means "let them strike/cast their khimars over their jayb-openings" — i.e., take the cloth that was hanging behind and pull it **forward and down across the chest opening**. This phrasing only makes sense *because the khimar was already on the head but the chest was exposed.* This is the linguistic fingerprint of the pre-Islam → Islam transition.

### A.2 — Tabarruj (تبرّج): public display of charms

*Tabarruj* is the Quranic term (33:33: *wa lā tabarrajna tabarruj al-jāhiliyyat al-ūlā* — "do not display yourselves as in the former Jahiliyyah") for the pre-Islamic custom of women **ostentatiously exhibiting beauty, jewelry, and adornment** in public.

- Classical exegetes describe *tabarruj* as a woman "putting a khimar on her head but **not tying it properly**, so her necklaces, earrings and neck could be seen." (reported in Tafsir Ibn Kathir / al-Tabari on 33:33.)
- **Mujahid ibn Jabr** (early mufassir) described women "emerging from their homes to walk directly in front of unrelated men, deliberately showcasing their beauty without restraint," sometimes with an exaggerated/swaying gait. [reported in classical tafseer of 33:33]
- The "former Jahiliyyah" framing in 33:33 confirms that the *contrast* Islam draws is explicitly **against a pre-existing dress culture of display**, not against simple nakedness.

### A.3 — Status distinctions in Jahiliyyah dress (free vs. slave women)

- In pre-Islamic and very-early-Islamic Arabia, **free women and slave women dressed differently**, and slave women in particular did **not** cover the way free women did. This class-distinction-by-dress is the direct background to **33:59** (the *jilbab* verse): free believing women were told to draw their outer garment around them **"so that they may be recognized [as free, respectable women] and not be molested."** Classical tafseer (Ibn Kathir on 33:59) states that the disbelieving men "used to pester" slave-girls who went uncovered, and the jilbab was the visual signal that distinguished a free woman from a slave so harassers would leave her alone.
- This mirrors the *exact same logic* found centuries earlier in Assyrian, Greek, and Persian law (Section B): the veil as a **legible social signal of "respectable / protected" status.**

**Bottom line for A:** Pre-Islam, the cloth existed but signaled and even enabled *display*; Islam kept the cloth and inverted its function to *modesty*, while also (33:59) turning it into a protective status-marker for free women.

## B. Veiling BEFORE & AROUND Islam in the Wider Near East
*(Establishing that veiling is a Near-Eastern / Late-Antique inheritance, not an Arab or "desi" invention)*

### B.1 — Middle Assyrian Law (c. 1400–1100 BCE) — the earliest legal veiling code

The **oldest known written law on veiling** is in the Middle Assyrian Law Code, roughly **two millennia before Islam**. It made veiling a **class-and-respectability marker enforced by law**:

- **Mandatory for respectable free women:** *"A wife-of-a-man, or widows, or Assyrian women who go out into the main thoroughfare shall not have their heads bare."*
- **Forbidden to the publicly available:** *"A prostitute shall not veil herself, her head shall be bare."* Violators faced beatings and hot pitch poured on the head.
- **Forbidden to slaves:** *"Slave-women shall not veil themselves"*; a man who saw a veiled slave-woman was to seize her, bring her to the palace entrance, and **"they shall cut off her ears."**

This shows veiling functioned as a **"marker of aristocratic rank"** that **"differentiate[d] between 'respectable' women and those who were publicly available."** (Wikipedia, *Veil*, citing the Middle Assyrian Law tablets; corroborated by academic veiling studies.)

> **Note the parallel:** The Assyrian distinction "free woman veils / slave does not" is the *same structural logic* later reflected in Quran 33:59 (jilbab distinguishes free believing women). This is not coincidental copying but a shared Near-Eastern social grammar of dress.

### B.2 — Ancient Greece (c. 550–323 BCE)

- "Respectable women in classical Greek society were expected to seclude themselves and wear clothing that concealed them from the eyes of strange men." (Wikipedia, *Veil*.)
- **Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones, *Aphrodite's Tortoise: The Veiled Woman of Ancient Greece* (2003):** marshals Greek literary and artistic evidence to show that **full veiling of head and face was commonplace** among Greek women. He links it to the concept of ***aidos*** (modesty/shame as a social virtue): the veil was a "container for and protector of feminine *aidos*." The veil (nicknamed *tegidion*, "a little roof") was "an ingenious compromise that allowed women to circulate in public while maintaining the ideal of a house-bound existence."

### B.3 — Rome

- For Roman **matrons**, the veil (and the *palla* mantle drawn over the head) symbolized **pudicitia** (chastity/virtue), especially in the Augustan period.
- A married woman's veil also signaled "the husband's authority over his wife"; the deep-yellow bridal veil, the ***flammeum***, was central to the wedding rite. The *Vestal Virgins* (priestesses of Vesta) also wore ritual head-coverings. (Wikipedia, *Veil*.)

### B.4 — Byzantine Christian world

- "Christian Byzantine literature expressed rigid norms pertaining to veiling of women, which have been influenced by Persian traditions." (Wikipedia, *Veil*.)
- Early-Christian veiling has its own scriptural anchor (1 Corinthians 11), and married women's head-covering was a long-standing Mediterranean Christian norm — another stream of the same Late-Antique practice that surrounded Arabia.

### B.5 — Sasanian Persia & Mesopotamia

- Veiling in the **Sasanian Persian** empire was likewise a sign of elite/respectable status, and Byzantine norms were "influenced by Persian traditions."
- **Veiled-priestess statuettes date as far back as ~2500 BCE.** "Elite women in ancient Mesopotamia and in the Byzantine, Greek, and Persian empires wore the veil as a sign of respectability and high status." (Wikipedia, *Hijab* / *Veil*.)

### B.6 — Jewish practice

- Rabbinical tradition held that **married Jewish women cover their hair** — an established norm predating Islam's emergence. (Wikipedia, *Hijab*.)

### B.7 — Academic syntheses (the scholarly consensus)

- **Leila Ahmed, *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992):** traces veiling to the pre-Islamic Near East. Quoting the Assyrian regime she summarizes: *"Wives and daughters of seigniors"* were obliged to veil; *"concubines accompanying their mistress had to veil; former sacred prostitutes, now married, had to veil; but harlots and slaves were forbidden to veil."* Ahmed stresses that before the modern era the veil meant **honor and the protected status of upper-class women**, not what later polemics made of it.
- **Fadwa El Guindi, *Veil: Modesty, Privacy and Resistance* (1999):** locates the veil's origin in **ancient Mesopotamia**, where "wives and daughters of high-ranking men of the nobility had to veil," regulated by **sumptuary law**. El Guindi documents that veiling **"is at least as old as the Assyrian laws on veiling, which date back to ~1500 BC"** and challenges the reductive idea that veiling is only about modesty/seclusion.
- **General scholarly statement:** "Available evidence suggests that veiling was **not introduced into Arabia by Muhammad, but already existed there**, particularly in the towns," where it was "associated with high social status." After the conquests, "the practice of veiling was borrowed from the elites of the Byzantine and Persian empires… and eventually it became widespread among Muslim women in cities throughout the Middle East." (Wikipedia, *Hijab*.)

**Bottom line for B:** Veiling is a **shared Near-Eastern / Late-Antique institution** — Assyrian, Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Persian, Jewish — predating Islam by up to two millennia and consistently functioning as a **status / respectability marker**. Islam inherited the *form* and gave it a *universal moral-spiritual rationale* (modesty before God, not aristocratic rank). It is therefore historically false to frame parda as a desi or even a narrowly Arab cultural invention.

## C. What Changed With the Quranic Commands (Early Islam)

### C.1 — The two key garment verses and their contexts

**(i) Surah al-Ahzab 33:53 — the "verse of the hijab" (the screen / curtain).**
*Asbab al-nuzul:* Revealed at the **wedding feast of the Prophet ﷺ and Zaynab bint Jahsh**. Some guests lingered talking after the meal in the small living quarters, causing the (intensely shy) Prophet ﷺ discomfort.

- **Anas ibn Malik (Sahih al-Bukhari):** "When the Messenger of Allah ﷺ married Zaynab bint Jahsh, he invited the people to eat, then they sat talking… The Prophet was extremely shy, so he went out… When the people had left, he came back, and when he was standing with one foot over the threshold and the other foot outside, **he placed the curtain (sitr) between me and him, and the Ayah of Hijab was revealed.**"
- The verse instituted **etiquette of entering the Prophet's home** and the rule that one should ask the Prophet's wives for things **"from behind a screen" (min warā'i ḥijāb)**. Note: here *ḥijāb* means a literal **partition/curtain**, and the ruling is specific to the Mothers of the Believers — it is *not* itself the general garment command.
- **'Umar ibn al-Khattab's prior wish:** Bukhari (via Anas) records that 'Umar had repeatedly suggested to the Prophet ﷺ that his wives observe hijab because "all sorts of people, good and bad, come to visit you" — one of the famous instances of revelation aligning with 'Umar's view.

**(ii) Surah al-Ahzab 33:59 — the jilbab verse (recognition & protection).**
*"O Prophet, tell your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers to draw over themselves [part] of their outer garments (jalābīb). That is more suitable that they will be **recognized and not be abused/molested**."*

- *Asbab al-nuzul:* Free Muslim women going out at night were being harassed by hypocrites/loiterers who claimed they "thought she was a slave-girl." The *jilbab* (a large outer sheet) made a free woman **visually recognizable as respectable and off-limits**, so harassers had no excuse. (Tafsir Ibn Kathir on 33:59.)
- **Ibn 'Abbas & 'Ubayda al-Salmani** described the practical method: drawing the jilbab from the top of the head down over the face, "leaving only one eye showing" — 'Ubayda even demonstrated it. (reported in Tafsir Ibn Kathir / al-Tabari on 33:59.) *(Scholars differ on whether face-covering is obligatory or recommended — recorded here as the classical report, not a fiqh ruling.)*

**(iii) Surah al-Nur 24:31 — the khimar verse (the chest).** See Section A: redirect the existing head-cloth forward *'alā juyūbihinna* (over the bosom-opening), explicitly to differentiate believing women from the *tabarruj* of the Jahiliyyah.

### C.2 — How the Sahabiyyat responded (the Ansar women)

The early Muslim women's response is the great "before/after" evidence:

- **Aisha (Sahih al-Bukhari 4759 — Book 65, *Tafsir*; in-book Book 65 Hadith 281; USC-MSA Vol. 6, Book 60, Hadith 282):** When the verse about drawing veils over the *juyub* (24:31) was revealed, the women **"took their waist sheets (izār), cut/tore them at the edges, and used the pieces to cover their heads (akhtamar)."**
- **Aisha praising the Ansar women (Sunan Abu Dawud 4102, and the version in Bukhari):** Safiyya bint Shayba reports Aisha mentioned the women of the Ansar, **praised them**, and said: *"When Surat an-Nur came down, they took the curtains, tore them, and made head-covers (veils) of them."* In the Abu Dawud wording: when the verse was revealed *"they tore the thickest/edges of their aprons (murūṭ / izār) and covered themselves with them."* Aisha's praise — *"May Allah have mercy on the women of the early Emigrants"* — is preserved in the same context (Tafsir Ibn Kathir on 24:31, citing Bukhari).

This narration is the linchpin: it shows (a) the women **already had cloth on hand** (their existing wraps), and (b) they **immediately reconfigured it** to cover the previously-exposed area — exactly matching the "khimar was worn back, now drawn forward" reading of 24:31.

### C.3 — Early Muslim women's garments — names & forms

| Garment | What it was | Function in the new code |
|---|---|---|
| **khimar** (خمار) | head-cloth / veil that **covers the head** | Pre-existing; now drawn forward over the *juyub* (neck + bosom) per 24:31 |
| **jilbab** (جلباب) | large **outer over-sheet / cloak** wrapped over the whole body | Drawn around when going out (33:59) so free women are recognized & safe |
| **dir'** / **qamīṣ** (درع / shift) | the **inner dress / long shirt** | Base garment; its front *jayb* opening is what the khimar now covers |
| **izār** / **mirṭ** (إزار / مرط) | **waist-wrapper / wrap-sheet** (also a body wrap) | The very cloth the Ansar women tore up to make head-covers (Bukhari 4759) |

The difference from Jahiliyyah was **not new fabrics** but **a new configuration**: the same head-cloth and wraps, now arranged to **conceal** (head, neck, chest, body) rather than to **frame and display** beauty and jewelry.

**Bottom line for C:** Early Islam took existing Arabian garments (khimar, izar, mirt, dir', jilbab) and (1) reoriented the khimar forward over the chest (24:31), (2) added the jilbab as an outdoor protective recognition-garment (33:59), and (3) instituted the *screen* etiquette for the Prophet's household (33:53). The Sahabiyyat — especially the Ansar women — adopted this **instantly and enthusiastically**, repurposing cloth they already had.

## D. Through Later Islamic History & Regional Variation
*(One principle, many cultural forms)*

### D.1 — Spread after the conquests

As Islam expanded into the former Byzantine and Sasanian lands (7th c. onward), Muslims encountered societies where **elite veiling was already entrenched**. "The practice of veiling was borrowed from the elites of the Byzantine and Persian empires, where it was a symbol of respectability and high social status, during the Arab conquests… veiling gradually spread to upper-class Arab women, and eventually it became widespread among Muslim women in cities throughout the Middle East." (Wikipedia, *Hijab*.) Thus the *cultural elaboration* of veiling (and its strong urban/elite association) was reinforced by the conquered regions' own pre-Islamic norms — the same Late-Antique inheritance described in Section B.

### D.2 — Regional garments — same principle, many forms

| Region | Garment(s) | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Arab world** (Gulf, Levant, Egypt) | **abaya**, **niqab**, **shayla / tarha** | *Abaya:* long loose outer cloak (worn by women; the term historically applied to a men's cloak too). *Niqab:* face-veil leaving the eyes; favored in Saudi Arabia, Yemen, the Gulf. |
| **Persia / Iran** | **chador** | A full-length open-front cloak held closed by the hands or tucked under the arm; often light-colored at home, black in public. Pre-Islamic Persian veiling roots (Section B.5). |
| **Afghanistan / Central Asia** | **burqa (chadari)**, **paranja** | *Burqa/chadari:* full body+face cover with an embroidered **mesh grille over the eyes** (the Afghan blue burqa). *Paranja:* a Central Asian (Uzbek/Tajik) cloak with a separate horsehair face-veil (the *chachvan*), worn into the 20th c. |
| **Ottoman / Turkish world** | **çarşaf**, **yaşmak**, **ferace** | *Yaşmak:* a fine double face-veil worn by Ottoman urban women; *çarşaf:* an all-enveloping outer cloak; *ferace:* an outdoor mantle. Distinctly Ottoman-Turkish elaborations. |
| **Mughal / South Asia** | **dupatta / chunni**, **chador** (subcontinental sense), **ghoonghat-style**, **burqa** | *Dupatta:* long shawl drawn over head and chest with shalwar-kameez (also worn by many non-Muslim South Asian women). The subcontinental *chador* is a large wrap. South Asian *purdah* (پرده, "curtain") gave the practice its common Urdu/Persian name. |
| **North & West Africa (Maghreb)** | **haik + aadjar**, **sefseri**, **melhfa/malahfa** | *Haik:* a large white/black rectangular wrap covering body and head (Algeria/Morocco), often paired with the small face-veil *aadjar*. *Sefseri:* the traditional Tunisian wrap. *Malhfa/melhfa:* the colorful Saharan/Mauritanian body-wrap. |
| **Southeast Asia** (Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei) | **tudung / kerudung / jilbab (local sense)**, **baju kurung** | *Tudung* (Malay) / *kerudung* (Indonesian): tailored headscarves (kerudung can have a small stiff visor). In Indonesia the headscarf is often called *jilbab* locally. Worn with the loose *baju kurung* dress. |

> **The point.** These garments differ enormously in **fabric, cut, color, name, and degree of face-covering** — because they grew out of *local climate, textile traditions, and aesthetics*. Yet they are all **cultural vehicles for one Quranic principle** (cover the body, draw the head-cloth over the bosom, and present a modest, recognizable public appearance — 24:31 & 33:59). The diversity itself is the refutation of "parda is just a desi cultural thing": if it were merely South Asian culture, it would not appear — under different names and fabrics — from Morocco to Indonesia, and it would not be anchored in 7th-century Arabian revelation and even older Near-Eastern law.

### D.3 — Colonial-era politicization of the veil (factual, brief)

By the 19th–20th centuries the veil became a **political symbol** in the encounter with European colonialism — used by *both* sides:

- **Egypt:** Reformist **Qasim Amin** (*Tahrir al-Mar'a*, 1899) argued against the veil and seclusion as obstacles to national progress — sparking a major Arab debate on women, modernity, and the veil. [secondary/encyclopedic]
- **Colonial Algeria:** France made **unveiling a deliberate strategy of domination**, casting the veil as proof of a "primitive" culture needing colonial tutelage; in **1958** the French staged public **mass "unveiling" ceremonies** (wives of French officers unveiling Algerian women).
- **Anti-colonial resistance:** **Frantz Fanon**, *A Dying Colonialism* (1959), chapter **"Algeria Unveiled,"** analyzed the veil as a battleground of identity: Algerian women weaponized the veil for the resistance — "the woman who sees without being seen frustrates the colonizer" — making the veil a symbol of *anti-colonial agency*, not just modesty.

The takeaway: much modern "the veil = oppression" framing is a **product of 19th–20th-c. colonial polemics**, not of the garment's actual history — which, as Sections A–C show, was about modesty, status-recognition, and protection within a far older Near-Eastern tradition.

## Flagged claims (use with caution)

- **[POPULAR / verify before quoting as primary]** The very specific "head-band tied in a knot at the rear, shirt-slit open at the front" description (Section A.1) is widely repeated in tafseer-aggregator sites attributed to Maududi/Tafhim and classical sources; the *substance* (khimar worn back, chest exposed) is solidly grounded in Ibn Kathir and the Bukhari/Abu Dawud narrations, but the exact wording should be checked against a printed tafsir before being quoted verbatim.
- **[VERIFY chain]** Sunan Abu Dawud 4102 wording ("tore the thickest of their aprons") is reliable but reproduced here from secondary aggregation; confirm against sunnah.com/abudawud:4102 before formal citation. Bukhari 4759 is confirmed directly from sunnah.com.
- **[CONTESTED — fiqh, not history]** The "leaving only one eye showing" report on 33:59 (Ibn 'Abbas / 'Ubayda) is a genuine classical report but represents *one* interpretive school on face-covering; whether niqab is obligatory vs. recommended is a live fiqh disagreement and should not be presented as settled.
- **[Secondary-source-only]** Some regional-garment micro-details (e.g., kerudung's "stiff visor," sefseri as "traditional Tunisian hijab") come from a single encyclopedic source; reliable enough for an overview, but cross-check for a published claim.
