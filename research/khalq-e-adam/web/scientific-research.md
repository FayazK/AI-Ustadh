## 1. Age of the earth and geological time

- **The earth is about 4.54 billion years old (± about 50 million years).** The first robust figure came from lead-isotope measurements on meteorites: Clair Patterson, "Age of meteorites and the earth," Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta 10 (1956): 230-237, gave 4.55 ± 0.07 billion years. The modern consensus review is G. Brent Dalrymple, "The age of the Earth in the twentieth century: a problem (mostly) solved," Geological Society, London, Special Publications 190 (2001): 205-221, DOI 10.1144/GSL.SP.2001.190.01.14, giving 4.54 ± 0.05 Ga. The oldest solar-system solids (calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions in meteorites) date to 4.567 Ga (Connelly et al., Science 338, 2012, DOI 10.1126/science.1226919). The oldest earth minerals are Jack Hills zircons at about 4.4 Ga (Wilde et al., Nature 409, 2001; Valley et al., Nature Geoscience 7, 2014). Status: settled to within about 1 percent.

- **How radiometric dating works, in one paragraph.** Some atoms are unstable and turn into other atoms at a fixed, measured rate that does not depend on heat, pressure or chemistry. Uranium-238 turns into lead-206 with a half-life of 4.47 billion years; potassium-40 turns into argon-40 with a half-life of 1.25 billion years; carbon-14 turns into nitrogen-14 with a half-life of 5,730 years. When a mineral crystallises from molten rock it locks in a known starting mixture (a zircon crystal, for example, accepts uranium but rejects lead). By measuring how much of the parent isotope remains and how much daughter isotope has accumulated, and knowing the rate, one calculates the elapsed time. Because different isotope pairs decay at different rates, the same rock can be dated several independent ways, and the answers agree. Carbon-14 only works back to about 50,000 years; uranium-lead works for billions. For human fossils the main methods are uranium-series on cave carbonates, argon-argon on volcanic ash layers above and below fossils, electron spin resonance and thermoluminescence on teeth and burnt flint, and cosmogenic nuclide burial dating. Textbook reference: Dickin, Radiogenic Isotope Geology, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2005.

- **Why the fossil record is reliable in broad strokes.** Fossils occur in a consistent order in rock layers worldwide (no rabbits in the Cambrian, no humans with dinosaurs), the order matches independent radiometric dates, and predictions made from the order have repeatedly been confirmed (for example, Tiktaalik, a fish-to-tetrapod transitional form predicted for Devonian rocks of about 375 Ma and then found there: Daeschler, Shubin and Jenkins, Nature 440, 2006). Where it is patchy: fossilisation is rare and biased toward hard parts and certain environments (river and lake sediments, caves), so soft-bodied organisms and rainforest species are under-represented; first and last appearances in the record are always later and earlier than the true origin and extinction of a lineage (the Signor-Lipps effect); and for hominins the total sample is small. There are only a few thousand hominin fossils older than 100 ka in existence, most of them isolated teeth and bone fragments. This is why new finds routinely shift dates by tens or hundreds of thousands of years without overturning the overall picture. Reference: Foote and Miller, Principles of Paleontology, 3rd ed., 2007; Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History Human Origins Program, humanorigins.si.edu.

## 2. The human family tree (hominins)

### 2.1 Timeline with key fossils

Dates are approximate and several are actively debated; the bushy, overlapping pattern is the settled part.

- **Human-chimpanzee divergence: about 6-9 Ma.** Molecular estimates vary with assumed mutation rate and generation time (Moorjani et al., PNAS 113, 2016, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1600374113; Langergraber et al., PNAS 109, 2012). Not a single date, and no fossil "missing link" is required by the theory.

- **Sahelanthropus tchadensis, Chad, about 7 Ma.** Brunet et al., Nature 418 (2002): 145-151. Whether it walked upright is contested: Daver et al., Nature 609 (2022), argue yes from a femur; Macchiarelli et al., Journal of Human Evolution 149 (2020), argue no.

- **Ardipithecus ramidus, Ethiopia, 4.4 Ma.** White et al., Science 326 (2009). Partial biped that still climbed.

- **Australopithecus afarensis, 3.9-2.9 Ma; "Lucy" (AL 288-1), Hadar, Ethiopia, 3.18 Ma, found 24 November 1974.** Johanson and Taieb, Nature 260 (1976); Kimbel and Delezene, Yearbook of Physical Anthropology 52 (2009). Brain about 400-500 cc (a third of ours), fully bipedal (Laetoli footprints, 3.66 Ma). The 50th anniversary was November 2024; Lucy and the 3.3 Ma child "Selam" were exhibited outside Ethiopia for the first time in Europe at the National Museum in Prague, 25 August to 23 October 2025 (nm.cz press release).

- **Earliest genus Homo: 2.8 Ma.** LD 350-1 jaw, Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia: Villmoare et al., Science 347 (2015): 1352-1355. New teeth from the same area show early Homo (2.78-2.59 Ma) living alongside a new, unnamed Australopithecus species (about 2.63 Ma): Villmoare et al., "New discoveries of Australopithecus and Homo from Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia," Nature, online 13 August 2025, print vol. 650 (12 Feb 2026): 374-380, DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-09390-4. A 2.6 Ma Paranthropus jaw from the Afar (Nature, January 2026) adds a third lineage in the same window. Human evolution is a branching bush, not a ladder.

- **Homo habilis, about 2.4-1.4 Ma, East Africa.** Leakey, Tobias and Napier, Nature 202 (1964). Brain about 600-700 cc; associated with Oldowan stone tools (the oldest stone tools, from Lomekwi, Kenya, are 3.3 Ma and predate Homo: Harmand et al., Nature 521, 2015).

- **Homo erectus, about 2.0 Ma to about 110 ka.** Oldest: DNH 134, Drimolen, South Africa, 2.04-1.95 Ma (Herries et al., Science 368, 2020); Dmanisi, Georgia, 1.85-1.77 Ma (first hominin outside Africa); youngest: Ngandong, Java, 117-108 ka (Rizal et al., Nature 577, 2020). Brain 700-1,100 cc; controlled fire by about 1 Ma (Wonderwerk Cave, Berna et al., PNAS 2012); Acheulean handaxes. Survived about 1.9 million years, longer than any other human species.

- **Homo heidelbergensis, about 700-200 ka, Africa and Europe.** Mauer jaw, Germany, about 609 ka (Wagner et al., PNAS 107, 2010). Often treated as the common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans, but the name is contested and some workers now prefer "Middle Pleistocene Homo" or Homo bodoensis (Roksandic et al., Evolutionary Anthropology 31, 2022). New: fossils from Grotte à Hominidés, Casablanca, Morocco, dated to 773 ± 4 ka by magnetostratigraphy and described as "basal to the Homo sapiens lineage," close to the last common ancestor of sapiens, Neanderthals and Denisovans: Hublin et al., "Early hominins from Morocco basal to the Homo sapiens lineage," Nature 649 (7 Feb 2026), DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-09914-y.

- **Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis), about 430-40 ka, Europe and western Asia.** Sima de los Huesos, Spain, about 430 ka, already on the Neanderthal line by nuclear DNA (Meyer et al., Nature 531, 2016); disappearance dated 41-39 ka across Europe (Higham et al., Nature 512, 2014). Brain size equal to or larger than ours (1,200-1,750 cc). Made composite tools, used fire and pigments, cared for injured, built structures deep in caves (Bruniquel, 176.5 ka: Jaubert et al., Nature 534, 2016), and probably buried some dead (La Chapelle-aux-Saints: Rendu et al., PNAS 111, 2014; contested by Dibble et al. 2015). A 2026 Nature paper on the genetic diversity of late Neanderthals in northwestern Europe shows small, low-diversity groups near the end (Nature, 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10625-1).

- **Denisovans, known mostly from DNA, about 200-40 ka, Siberia to Tibet to Southeast Asia.** Identified from a finger bone in Denisova Cave: Krause et al., Nature 464 (2010) (mtDNA); Reich et al., Nature 468 (2010) (nuclear genome). Xiahe mandible, Baishiya Karst Cave, Tibet, 160 ka, identified by proteins (Chen et al., Nature 569, 2019); Denisovan presence there 200-40 ka (Xia et al., Nature 632, 2024). Penghu 1 jaw, Taiwan, identified as Denisovan by proteins (Tsutaya et al., Science 388, April 2025, DOI 10.1126/science.ads3888).

- **Homo longi / "Dragon Man" (Harbin cranium), China, at least 146 ka.** Named as a new species from a nearly complete skull: Ni et al., The Innovation 2 (2021), DOI 10.1016/j.xinn.2021.100130. **In June 2025 two papers from Qiaomei Fu's group showed the Harbin individual is a Denisovan:** 95 endogenous proteins with three Denisovan-derived amino-acid variants (Fu et al., "The proteome of the late Middle Pleistocene Harbin individual," Science 388, 18 June 2025, DOI 10.1126/science.adu9677), and mitochondrial DNA recovered from dental calculus that falls within Denisovan variation (Fu et al., "Denisovan mitochondrial DNA from dental calculus of the >146,000-year-old Harbin cranium," Cell 188, 18 June 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2025.05.040). This is the first time a Denisovan has a face. Whether "Homo longi" becomes the formal species name for Denisovans is a naming debate, not a debate about the finding.

- **Homo juluensis ("big-head people"), China, about 220-100 ka: a 2024 proposal, contested.** Christopher Bae and Wu Xiujie proposed the name for large-brained fossils from Xujiayao and Xuchang (Bae and Wu, PaleoAnthropology 2024, and "Making sense of eastern Asian Late Quaternary hominin variability," Nature Communications, November 2024). They also suggested some fossils currently called Denisovan (Xiahe, Penghu) might belong to it. Status: a proposal under discussion; many paleoanthropologists have not adopted it, and the Harbin-Denisovan identification (above) complicates it. New dating of Xujiayao appeared in Quaternary Science Reviews in 2025. Treat as "proposed, not accepted."

- **Homo naledi, South Africa, 335-236 ka.** Berger et al., eLife 4 (2015); dating: Dirks et al., eLife 6 (2017). Small brain (about 500 cc) yet late date. Claims of deliberate burial and rock engravings (Berger et al., eLife reviewed preprints 89106 and 89102, 2023, revised version March 2025, with a supporting September 2025 update) remain contested; several reviewers judged the initial evidence inadequate. Status: burial claim = contested and under active review.

- **Homo floresiensis, Flores, Indonesia, about 100-60 ka (tools to 50 ka).** Brown et al., Nature 431 (2004); revised dating: Sutikna et al., Nature 532 (2016). About 1.1 m tall, brain about 420 cc. Possible ancestors at Mata Menge, 700 ka (van den Bergh et al., Nature 534, 2016; Kaifu et al., Nature Communications, August 2024).

- **Homo luzonensis, Callao Cave, Philippines, at least 67-50 ka.** Détroit et al., Nature 568 (2019): 181-186.

- **Take-away for the panel.** Between about 300 ka and 40 ka, at least six or seven kinds of human coexisted on earth (sapiens, Neanderthals, Denisovans, naledi, floresiensis, luzonensis, late erectus). Only Homo sapiens survives. This is settled.

### 2.2 Earliest Homo sapiens fossils and the pan-African model

- **Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, 315 ± 34 ka.** Hublin et al., "New fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco and the pan-African origin of Homo sapiens," Nature 546 (2017): 289-292, DOI 10.1038/nature22336; dating: Richter et al., Nature 546 (2017): 293-296. Modern face and teeth, but an elongated (archaic) braincase. This is the oldest fossil most researchers accept as early Homo sapiens.

- **Omo Kibish (Omo I), Ethiopia, older than 233 ± 22 ka.** Vidal et al., "Age of the oldest known Homo sapiens from eastern Africa," Nature 601 (2022): 579-583, DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-04275-8 (redating via volcanic ash; earlier estimate was 195 ka).

- **Florisbad, South Africa, about 259 ka** (Grün et al., Nature 382, 1996), assigned by some to early sapiens.

- **Herto, Ethiopia, 160-154 ka.** White et al., Nature 423 (2003): 742-747. Nearly modern skulls with cut marks suggesting mortuary practice.

- **Misliya Cave, Israel, 194-177 ka.** Hershkovitz et al., Science 359 (2018): 456-459, DOI 10.1126/science.aap8369. Oldest sapiens fossil outside Africa that is broadly accepted.

- **Apidima 1, Greece, at least 210 ka: contested.** Harvati et al., Nature 571 (2019): 500-504, argued a fragmentary braincase is sapiens; others argue it is Neanderthal or indeterminate (e.g., de Lumley et al., L'Anthropologie 124, 2020). Present as contested.

- **Skhul and Qafzeh, Israel, about 120-90 ka.** Long known; a 2025 study (Hershkovitz et al., L'Anthropologie 2025) proposed the Skhul I child shows mixed Neanderthal-sapiens features. Present as a proposal.

- **Model: "African multiregionalism" or pan-African origin.** Homo sapiens did not arise in a single small East African valley but across a subdivided, interconnected African population between roughly 350 and 150 ka: Scerri et al., "Did our species evolve in subdivided populations across Africa, and why does it matter?" Trends in Ecology and Evolution 33 (2018): 582-594, DOI 10.1016/j.tree.2018.05.005. Genetic support: Ragsdale et al., "A weakly structured stem for human origins in Africa," Nature 617 (2023): 755-763. A March 2025 genome model finds that all modern humans carry ancestry from two populations that split about 1.5 Ma and rejoined about 300 ka in roughly an 80:20 ratio: Cousins et al., "A structured coalescent model reveals deep ancestral structure shared by all modern humans," Nature Genetics 57 (18 March 2025), DOI 10.1038/s41588-025-02117-1. Status: the general pan-African picture is now mainstream; the specific 1.5 Ma / 300 ka model is one new result awaiting replication.

### 2.3 Behavioral modernity: symbols, burial, art

- **Pigment use is very old.** Ochre at Pinnacle Point, South Africa, about 164 ka (Marean et al., Nature 449, 2007). Engraved ochre at Blombos Cave, about 77-75 ka (Henshilwood et al., Science 295, 2002), and a 73 ka drawn cross-hatch on a stone flake (Henshilwood et al., Nature 562, 2018).

- **Shell beads: 150-100 ka.** Nassarius shell beads at Skhul (Israel) and Oued Djebbana (Algeria), 135-100 ka (Vanhaeren et al., Science 312, 2006); Bizmoune Cave, Morocco, 150-142 ka (Sehasseh et al., Science Advances 7, 2021, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.abi8620). Blombos beads about 75 ka.

- **Burial.** Skhul and Qafzeh, about 100 ka; the oldest well-documented African child burial, Panga ya Saidi, Kenya, 78 ka (Martinón-Torres et al., Nature 593, 2021). Neanderthal burials are probable but debated (see above).

- **Figurative art.** Leang Karampuang, Sulawesi, Indonesia: a narrative scene (human-like figures and a pig) dated to at least 51,200 years by laser-ablation uranium-series: Oktaviana et al., "Narrative cave art in Indonesia by 51,200 years ago," Nature 631 (2024): 814-818, DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07541-7. Chauvet Cave, France: two phases, about 37-33.5 ka and 31-28 ka (Quiles et al., PNAS 113, 2016). Hohlenstein-Stadel "Lion Man" and the Hohle Fels figurines, Germany, about 40-35 ka.

- **Was there a "cognitive revolution" about 50-70 ka?** Two camps. (a) Richard Klein argued a neural mutation around 50 ka triggered fully modern behaviour, explaining the Upper Palaeolithic "explosion" in Europe (Klein, "Archeology and the evolution of human behavior," Evolutionary Anthropology 9, 2000: 17-36; Klein and Edgar, The Dawn of Human Culture, 2002). Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens (2011) popularised a "Cognitive Revolution" at about 70 ka. (b) Sally McBrearty and Alison Brooks showed that the supposed modern package (blades, pigment, beads, long-distance exchange, fishing) appears piecemeal in Africa from 300 ka onward: "The revolution that wasn't: a new interpretation of the origin of modern human behavior," Journal of Human Evolution 39 (2000): 453-563, DOI 10.1006/jhev.2000.0435. **Current mainstream position: gradual and mosaic emergence in Africa, with a demographic and dispersal acceleration around 70-50 ka.** The Blombos, Bizmoune and Sulawesi finds all cut against a single sudden switch.

- **Note on Muslim writers.** Some Muslim authors link Adam (AS) to the appearance of behavioural modernity, symbolic thought or language, or to a later point, treating earlier hominins as non-Adamic humans. Positions are surveyed in Shoaib Ahmed Malik, Islam and Evolution: Al-Ghazali and the Modern Evolutionary Paradigm (Routledge, 2021), and in David Solomon Jalajel, Islam and Biological Evolution (2009), and Nidhal Guessoum, Islam's Quantum Question (2010). **This linkage is theological interpretation. Science has no data point labelled "Adam," and no archaeological horizon corresponds to a first ensouled human.** The scholars should say so plainly rather than claim the archaeology confirms a date.

### 2.4 Language origin: no consensus

- **Genetics.** FOXP2 was found through a family with severe speech impairment (Lai et al., Nature 413, 2001). Enard et al. (Nature 418, 2002) proposed human-specific changes under recent selection; Krause et al. (Current Biology 17, 2007) then found Neanderthals share the same two amino-acid changes, and Atkinson et al. (Cell 174, 2018) found no evidence of recent positive selection on FOXP2 in humans. So FOXP2 does not date language to the last 200 ka.

- **Anatomy.** The Kebara 2 Neanderthal hyoid (about 60 ka) is essentially modern (Arensburg et al., Nature 338, 1989; D'Anastasio et al., PLoS ONE 8, 2013), consistent with a speech-capable vocal tract in Neanderthals. Middle-ear reconstructions suggest Neanderthal hearing was tuned like ours (Conde-Valverde et al., Nature Ecology and Evolution 5, 2021).

- **Estimates range from about 50 ka to over 500 ka.** Klein and others: about 50 ka. Berwick and Chomsky (Why Only Us, 2016): roughly 100-80 ka. Dediu and Levinson (Frontiers in Psychology 4, 2013): language present in the common ancestor of Neanderthals and sapiens, over 500 ka. Miyagawa et al. (Frontiers in Psychology, March 2025) argue from population-split genetics that linguistic capacity existed by about 135 ka. **Honest summary: there is no fossil of a sentence; every date is an inference, and the field has no consensus.**

## 3. Genetics: what "Mitochondrial Eve" and "Y-chromosomal Adam" actually mean

### 3.1 Definitions and dates

- **Definition.** Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from mothers; the Y chromosome only from fathers. "Mitochondrial Eve" is the most recent woman from whom all living people inherit their mtDNA along an unbroken female line. "Y-chromosomal Adam" is the most recent man from whom all living men inherit their Y along an unbroken male line. These are MRCAs along one line of descent only. Everyone alive also descends from thousands of other people who lived at the same time as these two, through lines that mix male and female links. **The names are journalistic; they were coined by science writers, not by the 1987 authors.**

- **Founding paper.** Cann, Stoneking and Wilson, "Mitochondrial DNA and human evolution," Nature 325 (1987): 31-36, DOI 10.1038/325031a0, placed the mtDNA MRCA in Africa about 200 ka (their range roughly 140-290 ka).

- **Modern dates.** mtDNA MRCA: about 150-200 ka (Ingman et al., Nature 408, 2000: 171.5 ± 50 ka; Fu et al., Current Biology 23, 2013: about 157 ka, range 120-197 ka; Poznik et al. 2013: 99-148 ka). Y MRCA: Poznik et al., "Sequencing Y chromosomes resolves discrepancy in time to common ancestor of males versus females," Science 341 (2013): 562-565, DOI 10.1126/science.1237619, gave 120-156 ka; Karmin et al., "A recent bottleneck of Y chromosome diversity coincides with a global change in culture," Genome Research 25 (2015): 459-466, DOI 10.1101/gr.186684.114, gave 254 ka (95% CI 192-307 ka) using ancient-DNA calibration; the rare African A00 lineage pushed some estimates higher (Mendez et al., American Journal of Human Genetics 92, 2013: about 338 ka, later revised downward by others). **Working ranges: mtEve about 150-200 ka; Y-Adam about 200-300 ka, with wide error bars.** The two need not have been contemporaries and most estimates say they were not.

- **Four things these figures do NOT mean.** (1) They were not the first humans: tens of thousands of humans lived alongside each of them. (2) They were not the only ones alive, nor a couple. (3) They were not necessarily contemporaries. (4) The MRCA "moves": if one day every living carrier of a rare mtDNA branch dies without daughters, the mtDNA MRCA jumps forward to a more recent woman. The MRCA is a statistical property of the living population, not a fixed historical person. Reference: Takahata, "Allelic genealogy and human evolution," Molecular Biology and Evolution 10 (1993): 2-22; Relethford, "Genetic evidence and the modern human origins debate," Heredity 100 (2008): 555-563.

### 3.2 Effective population size and the single-couple question

- **Ne about 10,000.** Many independent methods estimate the long-term effective size of the ancestral human population at roughly 10,000 breeding individuals over the last million years (Takahata 1993; Harpending et al., PNAS 95, 1998; Li and Durbin, Nature 475, 2011, whose PSMC method shows Ne declining from about 10,000-15,000 toward a non-African bottleneck of a few thousand around 50 ka). Effective size is smaller than census size, typically by a factor of 2-10, so actual head counts were higher.

- **HLA argument (Ayala 1995).** Francisco Ayala, "The myth of Eve: molecular biology and human origins," Science 270 (1995): 1930-1936, argued that the great age and diversity of HLA-DRB1 alleles rule out any bottleneck below several thousand individuals. Bergström et al. (Nature Genetics 18, 1998) showed most DRB1 diversity is younger than Ayala assumed, weakening the specific HLA case, though the genome-wide Ne about 10,000 result rests on many other loci.

- **The Buggs / Venema / Swamidass exchange (2017-2018), stated exactly.** Dennis Venema (Adam and the Genome, 2017) wrote that population genetics rules out a first couple with "heliocentric certainty." Richard Buggs (Queen Mary University of London) asked, in a Nature Ecology and Evolution community post (October 2017), which published analysis had actually tested a bottleneck of exactly two individuals, as opposed to estimating long-term average Ne. A long exchange followed on BioLogos, on the Henry Center's Sapientia forum, and on S. Joshua Swamidass's Peaceful Science site (Swamidass, "Heliocentric certainty against a bottleneck of two?" 2017-2018; Steve Schaffner's coalescent simulations). **The outcome, which all parties broadly accepted:** (a) a single-couple bottleneck any time in the last roughly 500,000 years is excluded by the observed distribution of allele frequencies and linkage patterns; (b) current methods lose power before that, so a very brief single-couple bottleneck older than about 500-700 ka cannot be excluded or confirmed by genetics; (c) Venema acknowledged in 2018 that "heliocentric certainty" overstated the case for the deep past (BioLogos, 2018); Buggs summarised in "Adam and Eve: lessons learned" (richardbuggs.com, 18 April 2018). Swamidass later argued that a bottleneck of two before about 500 ka is scientifically unfalsified though biologically odd, and that it is in any case irrelevant to a recent Adam (see 3.3). Note for the panel: 500 ka is before the Jebel Irhoud fossils (315 ka), so a genetically undetectable single couple would have to predate anything called Homo sapiens.

### 3.3 Genealogical versus genetic ancestry

- **Key paper.** Rohde, Olson and Chang, "Modelling the recent common ancestry of all living humans," Nature 431 (30 September 2004): 562-566, DOI 10.1038/nature02842. Using simulations with realistic geography and migration, they found that the most recent common genealogical ancestor of all living humans lived only a few thousand years ago (their models give roughly 2,000-5,000 years; their headline figure about 3,400 years, i.e. around 1,500 BCE, probably in Asia), and that the "identical ancestors point" (the time before which every person then living is either an ancestor of all living humans or of none) lies roughly 5,000-15,000 years ago (headline figure about 7,400 years). Chang's earlier theorem for a randomly mating population: MRCA at about log2(N) generations (Chang, Advances in Applied Probability 31, 1999).

- **Why: pedigree collapse.** Each of us has 2 parents, 4 grandparents, 8 great-grandparents; 30 generations back (about 750-900 years) that formula gives over one billion slots, more than the world population then. So the same ancestors fill many slots, and lines of descent from any one person spread through the whole population in a few dozen generations, especially once migration links continents.

- **Genealogical is not genetic.** Most of your genealogical ancestors from even 1,000 years ago passed you no DNA at all; a genome is inherited in a limited number of chunks (Donnelly, Theoretical Population Biology 23, 1983; Coop lab, "How many genetic ancestors do I have?" gcbias.org, 2013). Therefore genetics cannot see, and cannot exclude, a specific couple a few thousand years ago who are genealogical ancestors of everyone alive today, provided they lived within a larger population.

- **Why this matters for models of a recent specially-created couple.** S. Joshua Swamidass, The Genealogical Adam and Eve: The Surprising Science of Universal Ancestry (IVP Academic, December 2019), argues that a de novo created couple living roughly 6,000-10,000 years ago in the Near East, whose descendants intermarried with humans outside the garden, would today be genealogical ancestors of everyone and would leave no detectable genetic signature; the model is compatible with all the genetic evidence above and with evolution of the surrounding population. This is a philosophical-theological model built on real population genetics. Muslim scholars may or may not want it (it requires humans outside Adam's line), but they should know it exists and that mainstream geneticists who reviewed it (e.g., in the Sapientia forum and Peaceful Science) agreed the genetics does not rule it out. The two things genetics does rule out are: a single couple as sole ancestors within the last 500 ka, and any couple as the sole source of present human genetic diversity.

### 3.4 Neanderthal and Denisovan admixture

- **Neanderthal ancestry in non-Africans: about 1-2 percent.** Green et al., "A draft sequence of the Neandertal genome," Science 328 (2010): 710-722, DOI 10.1126/science.1188021 (1-4 percent); Prüfer et al., Nature 505 (2014): 43-49 (1.5-2.1 percent). Africans carry small amounts via back-migration (Chen et al., Cell 180, 2020).

- **Denisovan ancestry: up to about 3-6 percent in Papuans, Aboriginal Australians and some Melanesians**, smaller traces across East and South Asia (Reich et al., Nature 468, 2010, gave 4-6 percent; later work gives 2-4 percent for most Papuan groups; Browning et al., Cell 173, 2018, found two distinct Denisovan sources). Tibetan high-altitude EPAS1 variant is Denisovan-derived (Huerta-Sánchez et al., Nature 512, 2014).

- **Timing of Neanderthal admixture: a single main period about 50.5-43.5 ka, average about 47 ka, lasting roughly 7,000 years.** Sümer et al., "Earliest modern human genomes constrain timing of Neanderthal admixture," Nature 638 (December 2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-08420-x (from 45 ka genomes at Ranis, Germany, and Zlatý kůň, Czechia); Iasi et al., "Neanderthal ancestry through time: insights from genomes of ancient and present-day humans," Science 386 (December 2024), DOI 10.1126/science.adq3010.

- **Fertile hybrids are documented.** Oase 1, Romania, about 40 ka, had a Neanderthal ancestor 4-6 generations back (Fu et al., Nature 524, 2015). "Denny," Denisova Cave, about 90 ka, had a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father (Slon et al., Nature 561, 2018). **Implication: Homo sapiens interbred with at least two other human lineages and the offspring reproduced. Whether they were therefore "one species" is a definitional question; the biological fact is settled.**

### 3.5 Out of Africa, and the Toba hypothesis

- **Main dispersal about 70-50 ka.** All non-African genomes derive mostly from a single dispersal around 70-50 ka (Mallick et al., Nature 538, 2016; Pagani et al., Nature 538, 2016; Nielsen et al., "Tracing the peopling of the world through genomics," Nature 541, 2017). Y-chromosome founder lineages outside Africa cluster at 47-52 ka (Karmin et al. 2015). Earlier excursions left fossils but little or no surviving genetic legacy: Misliya 194-177 ka, Skhul/Qafzeh 120-90 ka, Al Wusta finger bone, Saudi Arabia, about 88 ka (Groucutt et al., Nature Ecology and Evolution 2, 2018). Australia: Madjedbebe at about 65 ka (Clarkson et al., Nature 547, 2017) is contested; 50-45 ka is widely accepted.

- **Toba "bottleneck" hypothesis: now weak.** The Youngest Toba eruption, Sumatra, about 74 ka, was proposed to have caused a volcanic winter and a human population crash (Ambrose, Journal of Human Evolution 34, 1998). Against it: Lake Malawi sediments show no major climate collapse (Lane et al., PNAS 110, 2013); continuous human occupation across the Toba ash at Pinnacle Point, South Africa (Smith et al., Nature 555, 2018); and people thriving through the eruption at Shinfa-Metema 1, Ethiopia (Kappelman et al., Nature 628, March 2024, DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07208-3). The reduced Ne of non-Africans reflects the founder effect of leaving Africa in small groups, not Toba. Status: largely abandoned as a cause of a global bottleneck.

### 3.6 Diversity and "race"

- **Genetic diversity is greatest in Africa** and declines with distance from Africa (Tishkoff et al., Science 324, 2009; 1000 Genomes Project Consortium, Nature 526, 2015; Bergström et al., Science 367, 2020). About 85-90 percent of human genetic variation is found within any local population, not between continents (Lewontin, Evolutionary Biology 6, 1972; Rosenberg et al., Science 298, 2002).

- **"Race" is not a biological category.** American Association of Physical Anthropologists (now AABA), "AAPA Statement on Race and Racism," 27 March 2019, physanth.org: "Race does not provide an accurate representation of human biological variation... racial groupings do not appear in nature... race is a social construct."

## 4. Civilizations: when did complex society begin?

- **Agriculture: about 12-10 ka, Fertile Crescent first, then independently in China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Africa.** Wild cereals were harvested at Ohalo II, Israel, 23 ka (Snir et al., PLoS ONE 10, 2015), but domesticated wheat, barley, lentils, sheep and goats appear about 10.5-9.5 ka cal BP in the Levant, Anatolia and Zagros (Zeder, Current Anthropology 52, 2011; Larson et al., PNAS 111, 2014).

- **Göbekli Tepe, Turkey, about 9500-8000 BCE.** Monumental T-pillar enclosures built by pre-agricultural or proto-agricultural people: Klaus Schmidt, Sie bauten die ersten Tempel (2006); Dietrich et al., Antiquity 86 (2012): 674-695. Current excavators (Clare, 2020 onward) stress it was also a settlement, not only a "temple."

- **Çatalhöyük, Turkey, about 7100-5950 BCE** (often rounded to 7500 BCE), a dense town of several thousand: Hodder, Çatalhöyük: The Leopard's Tale (2006).

- **Mesopotamian cities: Uruk period about 4000-3100 BCE**; Uruk itself reached perhaps 40,000 people by 3100 BCE. **Writing: proto-cuneiform at Uruk about 3350-3200 BCE** (Nissen, Damerow and Englund, Archaic Bookkeeping, 1993); Egyptian labels at Abydos tomb U-j about 3320 BCE (Dreyer, Umm el-Qaab I, 1998). Which came first is unresolved.

- **Egypt Old Kingdom about 2686-2181 BCE** (Shaw, ed., The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt, 2000); Great Pyramid about 2560 BCE. **Indus Valley mature phase about 2600-1900 BCE** (Kenoyer, Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization, 1998; Possehl, The Indus Civilization, 2002). Also independent: Norte Chico, Peru, about 3000 BCE; Liangzhu, China, about 3300-2300 BCE.

- **No evidence for a technologically advanced "lost civilization" before the Holocene.** Graham Hancock (Fingerprints of the Gods, 1995; Netflix "Ancient Apocalypse," released 11 November 2022) claims an Ice Age civilization destroyed about 12,800 years ago. The Society for American Archaeology's open letter to Netflix (30 November 2022, documents.saa.org) states that after more than a century of investigation there is "no archaeological evidence" for "an advanced, global Ice Age civilization," and asked that the series be reclassified as fiction. What archaeologists do find from that period are hunter-gatherer camps, Natufian villages, and sites like Göbekli Tepe, none with metallurgy, writing, cities or agriculture. Every hunter-gatherer site of the Pleistocene excavated to date is consistent with this.

- **What a geologist would expect to find, and the honest scientific treatment.** Schmidt and Frank, "The Silurian hypothesis: would it be possible to detect an industrial civilization in the geological record?" International Journal of Astrobiology 18 (2019): 142-150 (online April 2018), DOI 10.1017/S1473550418000095. They conclude that a short-lived industrial civilization millions of years ago would leave few artefacts but should leave geochemical signatures: carbon-isotope shifts, synthetic compounds and plastics, spikes in rare-earth and radioactive elements, mass-extinction pulses. Some past events (the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, 56 Ma) superficially resemble such a signature but have natural explanations. No signature attributable to technology has been found. For the last 100,000 years the record is far better, and an industrial or metal-using civilization would be plainly visible: mines, slag, smelting pollution in ice cores (Roman lead is visible in Greenland ice: Hong et al., Science 265, 1994), quarries, roads, domesticated animal bones. Their absence before the Holocene is strong evidence, not merely absence of evidence.

- **How many humans were alive.** HYDE 3.2 estimates 4.4 million people at 10,000 BCE (upper estimate 8.9 million): Klein Goldewijk, Beusen, Doelman and Stehfest, "Anthropogenic land use estimates for the Holocene, HYDE 3.2," Earth System Science Data 9 (2017): 927-953, DOI 10.5194/essd-9-927-2017. The US Census Bureau's compilation of historical estimates (McEvedy and Jones 1978; Biraben, Population 34, 1979; Deevey 1960; Thomlinson 1975) gives a range of 1-10 million at 10,000 BCE, and 5-20 million at 5,000 BCE (census.gov, "Historical Estimates of World Population"). Reaching 1 billion took until about 1800 CE.

## 5. Isolated peoples ("the area between Malaysia and India")

- **Where.** The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are an Indian union territory in the Bay of Bengal / Andaman Sea, closer to Myanmar and to Sumatra (Indonesia) than to mainland India (Port Blair to Chennai is about 1,200 km; Great Nicobar to Sumatra is about 150 km). The Malacca Strait and Malaysia lie to the southeast. This is the region meant by "between Malaysia and India."

- **The Sentinelese of North Sentinel Island (about 60 km²).** The world's most isolated people. Population is unknown: the 2011 Census of India recorded 15 people seen from a boat; anthropologists estimate 50-200, with some estimates up to 500 (Census of India 2011; Pandya, "Sentinelese," in Andaman and Nicobar Tribal Research Institute publications; Survival International, survivalinternational.org/tribes/sentinelese). They hunt, fish and gather, use bows, and reject contact. The Anthropological Survey of India (T. N. Pandit) made gift-dropping visits from 1967; the first peaceful face-to-face contact was 4 January 1991; India adopted an "eyes-on, hands-off" policy after 1996-97 and stopped visits. On 16-17 November 2018 the American missionary John Allen Chau landed illegally and was killed; seven fishermen who ferried him were arrested; the body was not recovered (Indian police statements; BBC, 21 November 2018). In late March 2025 a US national, Mykhailo Polyakov, was arrested after landing on the island and leaving a can of Diet Coke and a coconut (Andaman police; BBC and Guardian, April 2025).

- **Legal protection.** The Andaman and Nicobar Islands (Protection of Aboriginal Tribes) Regulation, 1956 (ANPATR), bars entry to tribal reserves and any photography or contact; penalties were strengthened in 2012. A 5 km exclusion zone around North Sentinel exists under the regulation. In 2018 the Restricted Area Permit requirement was relaxed for 29 islands, including North Sentinel, then effectively restored after the Chau incident.

- **Other Andamanese and Nicobarese peoples (2011 Census unless noted).** Jarawa: about 380 in 2011, estimated 250-400 today, in a 1,000 km² reserve on South and Middle Andaman, in regular but limited contact since 1998; the Andaman Trunk Road through their reserve is a documented threat (Supreme Court of India orders 2002, 2013). Onge: 101 in 2011, on Little Andaman, roughly 130 today. Great Andamanese: 59 (2020), on Strait Island; ten distinct Great Andamanese tribes of the 19th century have collapsed into one small community, and their language (Aka-Bo, last speaker Boa Sr., died 2010) is nearly gone (Abbi, Voices from the Lost Horizon, 2021). Shompen: about 229 in 2011, in the interior of Great Nicobar, semi-nomadic, with limited contact; the Government of India's Great Nicobar mega-project (transhipment port, airport, township; NITI Aayog 2021) is opposed by anthropologists, 39 of whom warned in an open letter of February 2024 that it could be a "death sentence" for the Shompen. Nicobarese: about 30,000, settled and in full contact.

- **Genetics: descendants of the early Out-of-Africa dispersal, isolated for tens of thousands of years.** The Onge and Jarawa carry mtDNA lineages M31 and M32 found nowhere else, estimated to have separated from other Asian lineages roughly 50-65 ka, that is, shortly after the main dispersal (Thangaraj et al., "Genetic affinities of the Andaman Islanders, a vanishing human population," Current Biology 13, 2003: 86-93; Thangaraj et al., "Reconstructing the origin of Andaman Islanders," Science 308, 2005: 996). Whole genomes show the Andamanese are a deeply divergent branch of the same wave that peopled Asia, not a separate earlier migration (Mondal et al., Nature Genetics 48, 2016, whose additional claim of an unknown hominin contribution was rebutted by Skoglund et al., Nature Genetics 50, 2018). Their Y-chromosome haplogroup D is shared with Tibetans and the Ainu of Japan, another relic distribution. **Caution:** the islands' archaeological occupation is directly dated only to about 2,200 years (Cooper, Archaeology and History of the Andaman Islands, 2002); the "tens of thousands of years" is a genetic inference about ancestry, and the Sentinelese themselves have never been sampled.

- **Uncontacted peoples worldwide.** Survival International's October 2025 report, Uncontacted Peoples: At the Edge of Survival (uncontactedpeoples.org), counts at least 196 uncontacted groups in 10 countries: about 95 percent in the Amazon basin (124 in Brazil alone), plus groups in Peru, Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Venezuela, Paraguay, India (two: the Sentinelese and the Shompen), Indonesia (four, in West Papua) and Papua New Guinea. Earlier Survival estimates said "more than 100." Brazil's FUNAI lists about 114 "records" of isolated peoples, of which about 28 are confirmed (FUNAI, 2023).

## 6. Consciousness, the soul, and science

### 6.1 The hard problem and the state of the science

- **The hard problem.** David Chalmers, "Facing up to the problem of consciousness," Journal of Consciousness Studies 2 (1995): 200-219: explaining which brain processes correlate with experience (the "easy problems") is different from explaining why any physical process is accompanied by subjective experience at all. This framing is widely, though not universally, accepted; Daniel Dennett and others deny there is a separate hard problem.

- **Leading scientific theories.** Global Workspace Theory (Baars, A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness, 1988) and its neural version, Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (Dehaene and Naccache, Cognition 79, 2001): a content becomes conscious when it is "broadcast" widely, especially via prefrontal-parietal networks. Integrated Information Theory (Tononi, BMC Neuroscience 5, 2004; Tononi, Boly, Massimini and Koch, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 17, 2016): consciousness is identical to a system's capacity for integrated information (phi), located in a posterior cortical "hot zone." Others: Higher-Order Theories, Recurrent Processing Theory, Attention Schema Theory, predictive-processing accounts (Seth, Being You, 2021).

- **The adversarial collaboration: neither theory won.** The Cogitate Consortium pre-registered opposing predictions of GNWT and IIT and tested them in 256 participants with fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG. Preliminary results were announced at the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness meeting in New York in June 2023, where Christof Koch conceded a 25-year bet to Chalmers that the neural correlates of consciousness would be found by 2023 (Nature news, "Decades-long bet on consciousness ends," 24 June 2023, DOI 10.1038/d41586-023-02120-8). The full paper: Cogitate Consortium, "Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness," Nature 642 (published 30 April 2025), DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1. Findings: conscious content was decodable from posterior cortex, as IIT predicts, and prefrontal cortex did not show the "ignition" at stimulus offset that GNWT predicts; but posterior areas did not show the sustained synchronisation IIT predicts. Both theories failed some of their own predictions. This is how the field itself summarises it.

- **The "pseudoscience" letter (2023).** On 16 September 2023, 124 researchers (including Dennett, Baars and LeDoux) published an open letter, "The Integrated Information Theory of Consciousness as Pseudoscience" (Fleming et al., PsyArXiv, 2023), objecting to press coverage of IIT and to its implications (that some simple systems, or fetuses, might be conscious). Koch, Seth and others replied that IIT is testable and had just been tested. Coverage: Nature news, "Consciousness theory slammed as 'pseudoscience'," 20 September 2023, DOI 10.1038/d41586-023-02971-1. Status: an unresolved dispute about method, not a settled verdict.

- **What neuroscience can and cannot say about a soul.** It can say: specific experiences depend on specific brain activity; damage, drugs, anaesthesia and sleep alter or abolish reportable experience in predictable ways; there is no measurement of anything non-physical. It cannot say: why there is experience at all (the hard problem), whether experience persists without a brain (no method exists to test this), or whether an immaterial soul exists. Absence of a measurement method is not a measurement of absence, but it also means "the soul" is not currently a scientific hypothesis. Most neuroscientists are methodological naturalists on this point; a minority (e.g., the late Nobel laureate John Eccles) argued for dualism.

### 6.2 Near-death experience research, with honest results

- **AWARE (2014).** Parnia et al., "AWARE: AWAreness during REsuscitation, a prospective study," Resuscitation 85 (2014): 1799-1805, DOI 10.1016/j.resuscitation.2014.09.004. Of 2,060 cardiac arrests at 15 hospitals, 330 survived and 140 completed interviews; 9 percent had experiences meeting the standard NDE scale; 2 percent described explicit awareness of their surroundings; one patient gave a verifiable account of auditory events during about three minutes with no heartbeat. No patient reported the hidden visual targets placed on shelves; none of the "visual awareness" cases occurred in rooms with targets.

- **AWARE-II (2023).** Parnia et al., "AWAreness during REsuscitation - II: a multi-center study of consciousness and awareness in cardiac arrest," Resuscitation 191 (2023): 109903, DOI 10.1016/j.resuscitation.2023.109903. 567 in-hospital cardiac arrests at 25 sites in the US and UK; 53 survived to discharge; 28 were interviewed. About 39 percent reported some perception of awareness without explicit recall, about 20 percent reported a "recalled experience of death" (life review, feeling of peace, perception of leaving the body), and about 3 percent (one patient) reported concrete auditory or visual awareness of the resuscitation. No participant identified the hidden visual images; one recalled the auditory stimulus. EEG was interpretable in 53 patients, and in some, near-normal brain rhythms (gamma, beta, alpha) appeared up to 35-60 minutes into CPR. **Honest reading:** these are real, reproducible reports, and the EEG finding is scientifically interesting; but the study does not show consciousness without brain activity (CPR delivers some blood to the brain), the "verified out-of-body perception" test failed again, and the authors themselves call for larger studies. Sceptical review: Greyson, After (2021) for the believing side; Blackmore, Dying to Live (1993), and Martial et al., Trends in Cognitive Sciences 24 (2020) for physiological accounts.

### 6.3 The 2023-2026 AI-consciousness debate, briefly and dated

- **August 2023:** Butlin, Long and 17 co-authors (including Yoshua Bengio and David Chalmers), "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness," arXiv:2308.08708, derive indicator properties from GWT, higher-order and recurrent-processing theories and conclude no current AI is a strong candidate but no technical barrier exists. Chalmers, "Could a large language model be conscious?" (Boston Review, August 2023; arXiv:2303.07103) gave a subjective credence of under 10 percent for 2023 systems, rising over a decade.
- **2024:** Jonathan Birch, The Edge of Sentience (Oxford University Press, 2024) argues for precautionary frameworks covering animals, neural organoids and AI.
- **24 April 2025:** Anthropic announced a "model welfare" research programme ("Exploring model welfare," anthropic.com); Kevin Roose (New York Times, 24 April 2025) reported researcher Kyle Fish's personal estimate of roughly 15 percent that a current model has some form of consciousness. **15 August 2025:** Anthropic gave Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 the ability to end persistently abusive conversations, citing uncertainty about moral status. **19 August 2025:** Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI) published "We must build AI for people; not to be a person," warning that "seemingly conscious AI" is a dangerous illusion to be engineered against. Philosophers and neuroscientists remain divided; IIT proponents (Koch) say current digital computers cannot be conscious in principle, GWT proponents say architecture matters, not substrate.
- **Status:** an open debate about credences and precautions. No scientific test for machine consciousness exists, for the same reason none exists for the soul: the hard problem.

### 6.4 Embryology: the factual milestones

Ages are post-fertilisation unless stated; obstetric "gestational age" adds about 2 weeks. Source texts: Moore, Persaud and Torchia, The Developing Human, 11th ed. (2020); Carnegie stages (Hill, embryology.med.unsw.edu.au).

- **Days 0-14:** fertilisation, cleavage, blastocyst, implantation (day 6-10), primitive streak (day 14-15). Twinning is possible until about day 14.
- **Day 18-22:** heart tube forms; **the heart begins to beat at about day 21-22 (Carnegie stage 10), roughly week 3-4**, before there is any nervous system; clinical ultrasound detects cardiac activity from about 5.5-6 weeks gestational age. Neural tube closes about day 28.
- **Day 40 (about 6 weeks, Carnegie stage 16-17):** embryo about 8-11 mm; beating heart, limb buds with hand plates, eye pigment, early brain vesicles; no cortex yet; reflex movements appear from about week 7-8 on ultrasound.
- **Week 8 (day 56):** organogenesis essentially complete; all major organ systems present in primitive form; "embryo" becomes "fetus." External genitalia are not yet distinguishable.
- **Weeks 9-12:** external genitalia differentiate (visible on ultrasound from about 12-14 weeks gestational age).
- **Day 120 (about 17 weeks post-fertilisation, 19 weeks gestational age):** fetus about 13-15 cm crown-rump, about 200-250 g; **quickening (the mother first feeling movement) typically at 16-20 weeks gestational age** (about 18-20 in first pregnancies, 16 in later ones); the fetus has been moving since week 7-8, but the mother could not feel it.
- **Brain activity:** cortical neurons are generated 6-20 weeks; first synapses in the cortical plate about 8-10 weeks; thalamocortical connections form 23-30 weeks (Kostović and Judaš, Acta Paediatrica 99, 2010); EEG in premature infants shows intermittent bursts from about 24 weeks and more continuous, sleep-wake-like patterns from about 28-30 weeks; a 2005 systematic review placed the earliest plausible conscious pain perception at about 29-30 weeks gestational age (Lee et al., JAMA 294, 2005: 947-954), while a 2020 reappraisal argued for a possibility from about 12-13 weeks (Derbyshire and Bockmann, Journal of Medical Ethics 46, 2020) and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (2022) holds to 24 weeks or later. The 40/80/120-day framework of the hadith (Sahih al-Bukhari 3208, Sahih Muslim 2643) is a religious statement about the ruh; embryology has no measurement for ensoulment, and the scholars should not claim that any EEG or ultrasound milestone confirms or refutes it.

## 7. The "East = religion, West = reason" claim, as data

### 7.1 Where the major religions originated

| Religion | Region of origin | Approximate date | Continent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hinduism (Vedic roots) | Indus-Ganges plain | Vedas about 1500-1000 BCE | Asia |
| Zoroastrianism | Iranian plateau | about 1500-1000 BCE (contested) | Asia |
| Judaism | Levant (Canaan/Israel) | 1st millennium BCE | Asia |
| Jainism | Ganges plain | 6th-5th c. BCE (Mahavira) | Asia |
| Buddhism | Ganges plain / Nepal | 5th c. BCE (the Buddha) | Asia |
| Confucianism | China | 6th-5th c. BCE (Confucius, 551-479 BCE) | Asia |
| Daoism | China | 6th-4th c. BCE (Laozi, Zhuangzi) | Asia |
| Christianity | Judea/Galilee | 1st c. CE | Asia |
| Islam | Hejaz, Arabia | 7th c. CE (610-632) | Asia |

| Baha'i | Iran | 19th c. CE | Asia |

Every major world religion originated in Asia. Greek and Roman religion, Norse religion and the indigenous religions of Africa, the Americas and Australia are not "major" by adherents today but were local to their continents. Source: any standard reference, e.g., Britannica; Bowker, The Oxford Dictionary of World Religions.

### 7.2 Where philosophy and systematic reasoning originated

- **Greece** is the conventional origin of Western philosophy: Thales of Miletus (Miletus is in Asia Minor, present-day Turkey), about 585 BCE; Socrates, Plato, Aristotle (5th-4th c. BCE). But the same "axial age" (Jaspers, 1949) produced:
- **India:** the Upanishads (about 800-500 BCE); the Buddha's analytic psychology; the six darshanas including Nyaya (systematic logic and epistemology, Gautama's Nyaya Sutras, about 2nd c. BCE to 2nd c. CE), Samkhya (dualist metaphysics), Vaisheshika (atomism). Panini's Sanskrit grammar (about 4th c. BCE) is the earliest formal generative grammar (Staal, Universals, 1988).
- **China:** Confucius (551-479 BCE), Mozi (logic and consequentialism), Laozi and Zhuangzi, the School of Names (Gongsun Long), Xunzi's naturalism, Han Fei's legalism; the Mohist Canons contain systematic logic and optics (Needham, Science and Civilisation in China, vol. 2, 1956; Graham, Later Mohist Logic, Ethics and Science, 1978).
- **Persia:** Zoroastrian dualist ethics; later the Sasanian academy at Gundeshapur, which transmitted Greek and Indian medicine into the Islamic world.
- **The Islamic world (8th-14th c.):** al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), al-Ghazali, Ibn Rushd (Averroes), Ibn al-Haytham (experimental optics), al-Khwarizmi (algebra), al-Biruni, Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, Ibn Khaldun (historical sociology). The **Baghdad translation movement** of the 8th-10th centuries rendered nearly all of Greek philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy into Arabic, with state patronage under the Abbasids: Dimitri Gutas, Greek Thought, Arabic Culture: The Graeco-Arabic Translation Movement in Baghdad and Early Abbasid Society (2nd-4th / 8th-10th centuries), Routledge, 1998. Latin Europe recovered Aristotle largely through Arabic, via 12th-century Toledo (Gerard of Cremona) and Sicily, and read him with Ibn Rushd's commentaries (Burnett, "Arabic into Latin," in The Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy, 2005). The claim that reason is a Western, and religion an Eastern, possession is therefore false as history: Aristotle reached Paris through Baghdad and Cordoba.

- **The "Needham question."** Joseph Needham asked why modern science arose in 17th-century Europe and not in China (or the Islamic world), which had been ahead in technology for a millennium (Needham, The Grand Titration, 1969). Proposed answers include institutions (autonomous universities and printing), competition between small states, the Reformation's effects, colonial wealth, coal, and cultural attitudes to useful knowledge. No consensus. Relevant scholarly references, not verdicts: Joel Mokyr, The Gifts of Athena (2002) and A Culture of Growth: The Origins of the Modern Economy (Princeton, 2016), who stresses a Republic of Letters and a culture that valued useful knowledge; Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty (Crown, 2012), who argue inclusive versus extractive political and economic institutions explain wealth differences (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson received the 2024 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this work); Toby Huff, The Rise of Early Modern Science: Islam, China and the West (1993); George Saliba, Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance (2007), who dates the decline of Islamic science far later than the 12th century and ties it to economic shifts after 1500. Modern "first world" wealth correlates with industrialisation timing, institutions, colonial history, geography and energy access; it is not a measure of a civilization's rationality.

### 7.3 Scientific output of Muslim-majority countries today: figures

- **Global baseline (UNESCO Science Report 2021 factsheet, unesco.org/reports/science/2021):** world research spending was 1.79 percent of GDP in 2018 (up from 1.73 percent in 2014); 8.854 million full-time-equivalent researchers in 2018; only 37 countries spent 1 percent or more of GDP on R&D; China accounted for 44 percent of the growth in global research spending 2014-2018, the USA 19 percent, the EU 11 percent, South Korea 5 percent, India 4 percent; the G20 account for 88.8 percent of researchers, 93.2 percent of research spending and 90.6 percent of publications; high-income countries' share of publications fell from 69.3 to 62.9 percent over 2015-2019 as developing countries grew.

- **OIC share.** The 57 OIC member states have about 24-25 percent of world population. A Scopus/SCImago-based count for 2018 gives them 327,555 of 3,946,933 documents, i.e. 8.29 percent of world output, led by Iran (60,268; 1.52 percent) and Turkey (45,582; 1.15 percent) (bibliometric study reported via SCImago Journal and Country Rank data; see also Scientometrics, 2015, DOI 10.1007/s11192-015-1718-z). SESRIC (the OIC statistics centre) reported that OIC R&D intensity averaged about 0.41 percent of GDP against a world average of 1.78 percent, and that OIC countries accounted for about 1.8 percent of world gross R&D expenditure in the late 2000s (SESRIC, Research and Scientific Development in OIC Countries). Malaysia has the highest researcher density in the group (over 2,000 per million by 2016, UNESCO Institute for Statistics), Turkey the highest total spending, and Iran, Turkey, Pakistan, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia and Egypt produce roughly three quarters of OIC papers.

- **Growth.** Between 2000 and 2012, Iran's annual output grew from about 1,343 to 23,885 papers, Pakistan's from about 580 to 5,767, Malaysia's from about 867 to 8,122 (Scientometrics, 2015, DOI 10.1007/s11192-015-1718-z). Iran ranked around 15th-16th in the world by publication volume by the early 2020s (SCImago Journal and Country Rank). Saudi Arabia and Egypt each pass 30,000 papers a year; Pakistan passed 20,000 a year by 2020 (SCImago). Quality indicators (citation impact, Nature Index share) remain well below volume rank for most of these countries.

- **Nobel Prizes in the sciences to laureates from Muslim-majority countries or of Muslim background:** Abdus Salam (Physics 1979, Pakistan); Ahmed Zewail (Chemistry 1999, Egypt/USA); Aziz Sancar (Chemistry 2015, Turkey/USA); Moungi Bawendi (Chemistry 2023, born in Paris to a Tunisian family, USA); Omar M. Yaghi (Chemistry 2025, born in Amman, Jordan, to Palestinian parents, University of California, Berkeley, shared with Susumu Kitagawa and Richard Robson "for the development of metal-organic frameworks"; nobelprize.org, 8 October 2025). Five out of several hundred science laureates, for a quarter of humanity. The gap is real, recent (post-1500 CE), and institutional rather than civilizational, as the historical section shows.

## 8. Recent discoveries 2024-2026 (dated)

- **3 July 2024:** Oldest narrative cave art, Leang Karampuang, Sulawesi, at least 51,200 years (Oktaviana et al., Nature 631, DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07541-7).

## 9. Floods, deluge geology, and ancient lifespans

### 9.1 What geology says about a global flood

- **There is no global flood layer.** A flood covering all land within the last few hundred thousand years (the Pleistocene and Holocene) would leave a single, continuous, same-age layer of water-laid sediment over every continent, containing mixed remains of animals from every climate zone. No such layer exists. Instead, Quaternary deposits vary from place to place (glacial tills, wind-blown loess, river gravels, lake beds, desert dunes, cave sediments), each with its own local dates, and the ice cores of Greenland and Antarctica record continuous annual snowfall for 123,000 years (NGRIP) and 800,000 years (EPICA Dome C) with no interruption by seawater (North Greenland Ice Core Project members, Nature 431, 2004; EPICA community members, Nature 429, 2004). Lake Suigetsu in Japan preserves about 70,000 years of undisturbed annual sediment layers (Bronk Ramsey et al., Science 338, 2012). Tree-ring chronologies run continuously back about 12,000 years. Each of these would have been destroyed or interrupted by a year-long global flood.

- **There is not enough water.** The earth's total water is about 1.386 billion km³ (Gleick, Water in Crisis, 1993; USGS Water Science School), 96.5 percent of it already in the oceans. Covering the continents to the height of Everest (8,849 m) would need roughly 4.4 billion km³, about three times all the water that exists. The atmosphere holds about 12,900 km³ of water vapour, equivalent to about 2.5 cm of rain if it all fell at once. Melting every glacier and ice sheet would raise sea level about 66 m (IPCC AR6, 2021), enough to drown coastal cities but not hills. The water for a global flood has no source and no place to go afterward.

- **"Flood geology" is rejected by mainstream geology.** The modern movement dates from George McCready Price (The New Geology, 1923) and John Whitcomb and Henry Morris (The Genesis Flood, 1961), who tried to attribute the whole sedimentary record to one year. Its history is documented by Ronald Numbers, The Creationists: From Scientific Creationism to Intelligent Design (Harvard University Press, expanded ed. 2006). The scientific objections, beyond those above: the sedimentary record contains fossil soils, footprints, burrows, desert dunes, coral reefs and evaporite salt beds that form only slowly and on dry or shallow ground, stacked thousands of metres thick; fossils are sorted by age and lineage, not by density or habitat as a flood would sort them; radiometric dates from many independent isotope systems agree with the stratigraphic order; and the heat released by rapid plate motion or rapid radioactive decay would boil the oceans. Mainstream statements: National Academy of Sciences and Institute of Medicine, Science, Evolution, and Creationism (National Academies Press, 2008), which affirms a 4.5-billion-year earth and rejects flood geology; Geological Society of London, "Statement on Young Earth Creationism" (2008); and, for a readable book-length treatment by a working geologist, David R. Montgomery, The Rocks Don't Lie: A Geologist Investigates Noah's Flood (W. W. Norton, 2012), who concludes that the geology rules out a global flood but is consistent with catastrophic regional floods at the end of the last ice age.

### 9.2 Regional mega-floods that are documented and dated

- **Sea-level rise since the Last Glacial Maximum: about 120-130 m.** At the glacial maximum (26.5-19 ka; Clark et al., Science 325, 2009) sea level was about 120-134 m lower than today (Lambeck et al., "Sea level and global ice volumes from the Last Glacial Maximum to the Holocene," PNAS 111, 2014: 15296-15303, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1411762111). Most of the rise happened between 16.5 and 8.2 ka. It was not smooth: **Meltwater Pulse 1A**, about 14.65-14.3 ka, raised sea level 14-18 m in under 350 years, up to about 4 cm a year (Deschamps et al., Nature 483, 2012: 559-564, DOI 10.1038/nature10902). Coastal peoples everywhere lost land within living memory of a few generations. This is the geological backdrop to the Holocene, in which every recorded civilization arose.

- **Sundaland: the drowned shelf between Malaysia, Sumatra, Java and Borneo.** At the glacial maximum the Sunda Shelf was dry land of about 1.8 million km², joining the Malay Peninsula to Sumatra, Java and Borneo, and roughly doubling the land area of the region (Voris, Journal of Biogeography 27, 2000: 1153-1167; Sathiamurthy and Voris, Natural History Journal of Chulalongkorn University, Supplement 2, 2006). Dated cores from the shelf show the sea rising about 16 m in roughly 300 years at about 14.6 ka: Hanebuth, Stattegger and Grootes, "Rapid flooding of the Sunda Shelf: a late-glacial sea-level record," Science 288 (2000): 1033-1035, DOI 10.1126/science.288.5468.1033. The shelf was progressively flooded from about 18 ka until about 7 ka. This is the "area between Malaysia and India" in a literal geological sense: a subcontinent-sized lowland where people certainly lived, now under 20-80 m of sea. Popular books (Stephen Oppenheimer, Eden in the East, 1998) have built speculative "lost civilization" claims on this; the geology is solid, the civilization claims are not, and no Palaeolithic site has been excavated on the drowned shelf itself.

- **Persian Gulf: the "Gulf Oasis."** The Gulf is shallow (average about 35 m, maximum about 90 m), so at the glacial maximum it was entirely dry, a river valley where the Tigris, Euphrates and Karun flowed to the Strait of Hormuz. Sea water re-entered from about 14 ka and reached roughly today's shoreline between about 8 and 6 ka, with a small highstand around 6 ka that brought the sea close to Ur and Eridu (Lambeck, "Shoreline reconstructions for the Persian Gulf since the last glacial maximum," Earth and Planetary Science Letters 142, 1996: 43-57). Jeffrey Rose, "New light on human prehistory in the Arabo-Persian Gulf Oasis," Current Anthropology 51 (2010): 849-883, DOI 10.1086/657397, argues the exposed basin was a well-watered refuge for humans through the arid glacial period and that the sudden appearance of settled sites along the Gulf coast about 7.5 ka reflects people displaced by its flooding. Teller et al. (Quaternary International 68-71, 2000) had already suggested the Gulf's reflooding as a source of Mesopotamian flood memory. Status: the shoreline history is settled; the cultural-memory link is a reasonable hypothesis, not a demonstration.

- **Black Sea deluge: proposed 1997, now contested and probably much smaller than claimed.** William Ryan and Walter Pitman proposed that around 7,600 years ago (about 5600 BCE) the rising Mediterranean burst through the Bosporus and filled a Black Sea lake standing about 80 m lower, at up to 50 km³ a day for about 300 days, drowning about 100,000 km² and perhaps seeding the Near Eastern flood stories: Ryan et al., "An abrupt drowning of the Black Sea shelf," Marine Geology 138 (1997): 119-126; Ryan and Pitman, Noah's Flood (Simon and Schuster, 1998). Critiques: Aksu et al., GSA Today 12 (2002), found persistent Holocene outflow from the Black Sea, not catastrophic inflow; Yanko-Hombach et al., eds., The Black Sea Flood Question: Changes in Coastline, Climate and Human Settlement (Springer, 2007), compiled decades of Russian and Ukrainian data showing repeated gradual connections and a much smaller level change; Giosan et al., Quaternary Science Reviews 28 (2009), found the pre-connection lake stood only about 30 m below present sea level, not 80 m, cutting the flooded area by most of its extent; Aksu and Hiscott, Earth-Science Reviews (2022), review the evidence and conclude it is insufficient for a catastrophic flood, with no archaeological sign of people fleeing. Present as: a real reconnection about 9,000-7,500 years ago, magnitude and speed disputed, the dramatic version not supported by most recent work.

- **Mesopotamian flood deposits: real, local, and not simultaneous.** Leonard Woolley found about 3.5 m of clean water-laid silt in a deep pit at Ur in 1929 and announced it as "the Flood" (Woolley, Ur of the Chaldees, 1929); it dates to about 3500 BCE (late Ubaid to early Uruk). But it was not found across the whole mound of Ur, and not at all at nearby Eridu or at Uruk, so it records a local river flood. Other flood deposits occur at Kish (about 2900-2800 BCE), Shuruppak (modern Fara, about 2900 BCE) and Uruk (about 2900 BCE), all near the Jemdet Nasr to Early Dynastic I transition. Max Mallowan, "Noah's Flood Reconsidered," Iraq 26 (1964): 62-82, argued that the Shuruppak flood of about 2900 BCE is the historical kernel behind the Sumerian tradition, since the King List places the flood immediately before the First Dynasty of Kish and makes Ziusudra king of Shuruppak. These were destructive Euphrates floods over the flat southern plain, each covering a city and its hinterland. None is region-wide, and none is global.

- **Doggerland.** The southern North Sea was dry land connecting Britain to the continent, home to Mesolithic hunters; it was drowned by rising seas between about 10 and 7 ka, and part of it was struck by the Storegga Slide tsunami about 8,150 years ago (Bondevik et al., Boreas 26, 1997; Weninger et al., Documenta Praehistorica 35, 2008; Gaffney, Fitch and Smith, Europe's Lost World: The Rediscovery of Doggerland, Council for British Archaeology, 2009; Walker et al., Antiquity 94, 2020, showing parts survived the tsunami before final submergence).

- **Lake Agassiz and the Missoula floods (North America).** The glacial Lake Agassiz, larger than all the Great Lakes combined, drained catastrophically several times; its final outburst about 8,470 years ago released roughly 163,000 km³ into Hudson Bay and the Atlantic and is linked to the "8.2 ka" cold event (Barber et al., Nature 400, 1999: 344-348). An earlier drainage about 12.9 ka, possibly via the Mackenzie River, is one candidate trigger for the Younger Dryas cooling (Murton et al., Nature 464, 2010; contested). The Missoula floods, about 40 outbursts of glacial Lake Missoula between about 15 and 13 ka, carved the Channeled Scablands of Washington State (Bretz, Journal of Geology 31, 1923; Waitt, GSA Bulletin 96, 1985). These are the largest freshwater floods known, and they are regional.

- **The Zanclean flood: 5.33 Ma, far too early for humans.** After the Mediterranean dried out during the Messinian Salinity Crisis (5.97-5.33 Ma), Atlantic water broke through at Gibraltar and refilled 90 percent of the basin in months to about two years, with the water rising up to 10 m a day: Garcia-Castellanos et al., "Catastrophic flood of the Mediterranean after the Messinian salinity crisis," Nature 462 (2009): 778-781, DOI 10.1038/nature08555. The genus Homo did not exist for another 2.5 million years; Sahelanthropus lived in Chad. No human saw this flood.

### 9.3 Flood narratives worldwide

- **Mesopotamian texts, with tablet dates.** (1) The Sumerian Flood Story or "Eridu Genesis," hero Ziusudra of Shuruppak: Nippur tablet CBS 10673, Old Babylonian, about 1600 BCE (Jacobsen, "The Eridu Genesis," Journal of Biblical Literature 100, 1981: 513-529). (2) Atrahasis, Old Babylonian, the main copy written by the scribe Ipiq-Aya and dated to the 12th year of King Ammi-saduqa of Babylon, about 1635 BCE (Lambert and Millard, Atra-hasis: The Babylonian Story of the Flood, Oxford, 1969). (3) The "Ark Tablet," Old Babylonian, about 1750 BCE, with instructions for a round coracle ark (Finkel, The Ark Before Noah, 2014). (4) Gilgamesh Tablet XI, hero Utnapishtim, Standard Babylonian version, copies from the 7th-century BCE library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, identified by George Smith in 1872 (George, The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic, Oxford, 2003). (5) The Sumerian King List, Weld-Blundell prism (WB 444, Ashmolean Museum), about 1800 BCE, with the line "then the Flood swept over" between the antediluvian kings and the First Dynasty of Kish. Berossus (3rd c. BCE) preserved a Greek version with the hero Xisuthros. The Genesis account (Genesis 6-9) shares structure and details with these (a warned hero, a boat, animals, birds sent out, a sacrifice, a divine promise); the Quranic account (Surah Hud 11:25-49, Surah Nuh 71, and elsewhere) shares the core of a warner, a ship and a drowning of a disbelieving people, without the details of the Mesopotamian versions.

- **Near-universality, and the caution.** Flood myths are among the most widespread of all myth types, recorded from Mesopotamia, India (Manu and the fish, Shatapatha Brahmana), China (Yu the Great), Greece (Deucalion), the Americas, Australia, and the Pacific: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "flood myth"; Frazer, Folk-Lore in the Old Testament, vol. 1 (1918); Alan Dundes, ed., The Flood Myth (University of California Press, 1988); Witzel, The Origins of the World's Mythologies (Oxford, 2012). **Cautions the scholars should state:** (a) the distribution is wide but not universal: ancient Egypt, Japan and much of sub-Saharan Africa have no comparable deluge tradition, and many "flood myths" recorded after 1500 CE were collected by missionaries and may reflect Genesis; (b) floods are the most common natural disaster on earth, and every river-valley and coastal people has experienced catastrophic ones, so shared stories do not require a shared event; (c) some traditions do preserve real regional inundation: Nunn and Reid, "Aboriginal memories of inundation of the Australian coast dating from more than 7000 years ago," Australian Geographer 47 (2016): 11-47, DOI 10.1080/00049182.2015.1077539, document stories from 21 locations that match post-glacial sea-level rise. The comparative evidence is consistent with many local floods and with cultural memory of the post-glacial drowning of coastlines; it does not by itself demonstrate one global event, and the geology (9.1) rules that out.

### 9.4 Human lifespan: what is documented and what biology can say

- **The maximum documented human lifespan is 122 years 164 days:** Jeanne Calment, France, 21 February 1875 to 4 August 1997, validated from civil records (Robine and Allard, in Jeune and Vaupel, eds., Validation of Exceptional Longevity, Odense University Press, 1999). A 2018 claim that her daughter had assumed her identity was examined and rejected by demographers (Robine et al., Journals of Gerontology Series A 74, 2019). The second oldest, Kane Tanaka of Japan, died in 2022 at 119; the oldest man, Jiroemon Kimura, died in 2013 at 116. Roughly one person in a few million reaches 110.

- **Biology of the limit.** Human mortality roughly doubles every 8 years after age 30 (Gompertz, 1825). Normal human cells divide only about 40-60 times before senescence (Hayflick and Moorhead, Experimental Cell Research 25, 1961: 585-621), linked to telomere shortening; stem cells and repair systems also decline. Dong, Milholland and Vijg, "Evidence for a limit to human lifespan," Nature 538 (2016): 257-259, argued the ceiling is about 115 with 125 as an extreme outlier; Barbi et al., "The plateau of human mortality: demography of longevity pioneers," Science 360 (2018): 1459-1461, found mortality flattens after 105, so there is no hard wall, but the annual death risk at that age is about 50 percent, making ages beyond about 125 vanishingly improbable; Olshansky et al., Nature Aging 4 (2024), show gains in life expectancy slowing in the longest-lived countries. Other vertebrates live longer (bowhead whale about 200 years; Greenland shark about 270-500 years: Nielsen et al., Science 353, 2016), so long life is not biologically impossible in principle, but **no known mechanism in human biology allows a 900-year life, and no skeleton, tooth or text outside scripture records one.** Bone and tooth microstructure give age at death for ancient skeletons; the oldest reliably aged prehistoric individuals are in their 60s and 70s.

- **The scriptural long lives as an ancient Near Eastern pattern.** The Sumerian King List gives eight antediluvian kings reigns totalling 241,200 years (Alulim 28,800; Alalngar 36,000; En-men-lu-ana 43,200, all multiples of the sar, 3,600), then after the flood the kings of Kish reign 1,200, 960, 900 years and so on, decreasing toward historical lengths. Genesis 5 and 11 show the same shape: Adam 930, Methuselah 969, Noah 950, then Shem 600, Abraham 175, Moses 120, and Psalm 90:10's 70 or 80. The Quran (29:14) says Nuh remained among his people 950 years. Scholars of the Hebrew Bible and Assyriology read the great numbers as carrying meaning in a sexagesimal (base 60) numerical culture rather than as census data (Jacobsen, The Sumerian King List, University of Chicago Press, 1939; Dwight Young, "On the application of numbers from Babylonian mathematics to biblical life spans and epochs," Zeitschrift für die alttestamentliche Wissenschaft 100, 1988: 331-361; Carol Hill, "Making sense of the numbers of Genesis," Perspectives on Science and Christian Faith 55, 2003: 239-251). For the podcast: the honest scientific position is that these lifespans have no biological mechanism and no physical evidence; whether one accepts them rests on revelation, not on data. The scholars can say exactly that.

### 9.5 The "6,000 years" chronology

- **Where the date comes from.** Archbishop James Ussher, Annales Veteris Testamenti (1650), fixed creation at the evening before 23 October 4004 BCE by adding the father-to-son ages in Genesis 5 and 11 (Masoretic text), the reign lengths of Judah's kings, and a synchronism with the death of Nebuchadnezzar II in 562 BCE; his dates were printed in the margins of English Bibles from 1701 and became "the" biblical chronology in the English-speaking world (Barr, "Why the world was created in 4004 BC: Archbishop Ussher and biblical chronology," Bulletin of the John Rylands Library 67, 1985: 575-608). It was one of many: Bede, Scaliger, Kepler and Newton all computed creation dates within a few centuries of 4000 BCE; the Septuagint's longer genealogies give about 5500 BCE (the Byzantine world era begins 5509 BCE); the Jewish Anno Mundi count (Seder Olam Rabbah, 2nd c. CE) gives 3761 BCE. All of these are arithmetic on genealogies, not observations of the world.

- **Islamic tradition has no fixed date.** Classical Muslim chroniclers such as al-Tabari (Ta'rikh al-rusul wa'l-muluk, vol. 1) open with transmitted reports on the total duration of the world and the time since Adam (figures of about 6,000-7,000 years appear), presented as reports with varying chains, not as revealed doctrine; there is no Quranic date for Adam and no consensus figure. The scholars are free to say the Quran gives no chronology.

- **Contrast with the physical record.** Sections 1 and 2 above give: earth 4.54 billion years; anatomically modern-looking Homo sapiens by 315 ka (Jebel Irhoud); art by 51 ka; farming by 10 ka; Göbekli Tepe by 9500 BCE; cities and writing by about 3300 BCE, i.e. before Ussher's date for Noah's flood (2348 BCE) and within seven centuries of his date for creation. Egypt's Old Kingdom pyramids (about 2600 BCE), the Indus cities (2600 BCE), the Sumerian archives of Uruk and Shuruppak (3200-2600 BCE) and the continuous ice-core, tree-ring and lake-sediment chronologies all run straight through 2348 BCE without any sign of a world-ending flood. A 6,000-year chronology of the world is therefore not a scientific option; the scholars' choice is between treating the genealogies as literary and treating the physical record as illusory, and this file cannot help with that choice beyond stating that the record is what it is.
